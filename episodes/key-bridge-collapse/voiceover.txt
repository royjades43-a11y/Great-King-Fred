At 1:25 in the morning on March 26th, 2024, a loaded containership leaving Baltimore lost its electrical power. The Dali was 984 feet long, and about 3,200 feet from the Francis Scott Key Bridge. Within eight seconds, its engine had shut down, and its steering pumps had no power. The ship kept moving. Four minutes and nine seconds later, it struck a pier of the bridge. Thirteen seconds after that, the central spans were in the river. Six men from a road crew working on the bridge were killed. The cause had been on board since the ship was built. It was small enough to hold between two fingers. How does a ship that size lose everything at once? And why did a 47-year-old bridge come down in seconds?

The Francis Scott Key Bridge opened in 1977, carrying Maryland 695 across the mouth of Baltimore's harbor. It belonged to the Maryland Transportation Authority, the MDTA. It was a steel through-truss about 9,086 feet long, 185 feet above the shipping channel. In 2023, it carried 34,121 vehicles a day. Ships passed between Piers 17 and 18. From the edge of the 700-foot channel to either pier was only 200 feet. The Dali was Singapore-flagged, built in 2015 by HD Hyundai Heavy Industries, and managed by Synergy Marine. It had one main engine and one rudder. Its steering, the engine's cooling-water pumps and most of its lights ran on generator power. On the bridge that night, a Brawner Builders crew was filling potholes. An inspector walked the span, checking on drying cement.

The afternoon before, the Dali blacked out twice at the dock. A crewmember closed a generator's exhaust damper by mistake. Minutes later, a pump feeding fuel to another generator failed to restart. Afterward, the electrician switched to a transformer, and a breaker called HR1, that had not been used in at least seven months. Just after midnight, two pilots boarded. At about 12:36 the Dali got underway. By 1:08 its tugs had been released, as is normal in Baltimore. At 1:25, a breaker opened with no alarm. The ship's low-voltage power went dark. All three steering pumps lost power, and most of the lights went out. The bridge was 3.3 ship lengths ahead. Eight seconds later, the main engine shut down on low cooling-water pressure. It would not restart before the impact. The senior pilot asked, "Do we have steering?" The answer: "We have steering." But no steering pump had power. With no engine and the rudder stuck, the ship swung to starboard. The helmsman called it out: "Swinging to starboard, wheel on hard port sir." At 1:25 and 58 seconds, the electrician closed the breakers by hand. Power was back after 58 seconds. But the flushing pump, the only fuel supply for the two running generators, did not restart. The senior pilot phoned the pilots' dispatcher to get the Key Bridge shut down. The ship was 2,430 feet away. The pilot radioed a tug: "that's it, hammer down." The nearest was sixteen minutes away. At 1:27 and 2 seconds, the senior pilot ordered the port anchor. The bridge was 1,500 feet away. Two seconds later, the Dali went completely dark. Starved of fuel, both running generators dropped off the line. The senior pilot shouted for the bow thruster. It did not work; its pumps had tripped in the first blackout. And the anchor brake would not open. At 1:27 and 53 seconds, police at each end of the bridge were told to stop traffic. Both ends were blocked 48 seconds before impact. At 1:28 and 42 seconds, the anchor finally went down. Seven seconds later, the bow began to swing back to port. Too late. The pilot ordered full bow thruster. "No, it's not working." "It's not working." At 1:29 and 9 seconds, the Dali's starboard bow struck Pier 17 at 6.4 knots, with more than four times the sideways force the pier could resist. Thirteen seconds later, Pier 17 collapsed and the main spans fell. The inspector heard a "crumbling thunder noise" and ran north. He survived. Six men from the road crew were in their vehicles, on a break. They did not survive. A seventh escaped through his vehicle's open window, and was rescued at 1:55. That evening, the search became a recovery. The last of the six men was found on May 7th.

The National Transportation Safety Board, the NTSB, was on scene that day, with its chair, Jennifer Homendy. The voyage data recorder had stopped logging ship data at 1:25 and 3 seconds. Investigators rebuilt the final minutes from its audio, cameras and alarm logs. The flushing pump explained the second blackout. A January email from the previous chief engineer had described the arrangement. But it did not explain the first. The first began when breaker HR1 opened on its own, with no alarm. It was as if an operator had turned the switch. On April 29th, during testing, HR1 tripped again. Shipyard engineers traced it to a single wire: Wire 1, in Terminal Block 381. Homendy compared it to hunting for a loose rivet on the Eiffel Tower. In the lab, X-rays and microscopes found arcing damage on the wire's metal tip, called a ferrule, and on the clamp it sat in.

To understand the wire, go back to 2015, when the Dali was built. Each control wire got a heat-shrunk label band printed with its terminal number. The builder told the NTSB it had no specific materials, instructions or training for this work. On Wire 1, the band was slid too far down. It covered the ferrule's blue collar, so the wire could not be fully inserted. Done correctly, the band sits above the collar and the ferrule seats fully in the spring clamp. On Wire 1, its tip rested on the clamp's gate. That left an intermittent contact. Nobody noticed, partly because the breaker it served had sat unused for months. The last switchboard inspection, three days before the accident, found no abnormalities. Infrared thermal imaging, the NTSB found, may have caught the loose wire. So, at or just before 1:25, the wire let go. HR1 opened. The cooling-water pump stopped, the engine shut down, and the steering died. The breakers were set to manual. In automatic, power would have been back in about ten seconds, not 58. The NTSB's probable cause: a loss of electrical power, due to a loose signal wire connection stemming from the improper installation of wire-label banding. That explains why the ship hit the bridge. It does not explain why the bridge fell, or why six men died. In 1980, a freighter struck Florida's Sunshine Skyway Bridge, and 35 people died. By 1991, national guidance recommended checking older bridges for ship-strike risk. That same year, the 390-foot Blue Nagoya hit Pier 17's fender. The fender stopped it, and was rebuilt to its original design. The Dali was ten times its size. In 2006, at a harbor safety meeting, an MDTA representative stated that the Key Bridge was not designed to withstand contact by a large vessel. The MDTA never ran the recommended vulnerability assessment; no rule required it. The NTSB ran it afterward: a collapse risk almost 30 times the threshold for a critical bridge. The protective dolphins stood about 489 feet from the piers. The Dali touched none of them. And the workers. The inspector had swapped phone numbers with the police officers. But when the order came, the police officers did not call the inspector. Warned when the police were, the crew would have had one minute and 16 seconds, perhaps enough to drive to a part of the bridge that stayed up. Three failures. A wire never pushed home. A bridge never assessed. A crew never warned.

On May 13th, explosive charges cut the fallen truss off the Dali's bow. On May 20th, the ship was refloated. About 50,000 tons of material came out of the river. The Fort McHenry Channel fully reopened on June 10th. The Dali was repaired in China, and returned to service in January 2025. In March 2025, the NTSB issued urgent recommendations: 30 owners of 68 bridges in 19 states should calculate the same collapse risk. The final report asked for more, from a fix to the shipyard's banding procedure to ways of warning drivers and workers on bridges. The NTSB found that the quick actions of the pilots, the dispatcher and the MDTA police prevented a greater loss of life. Six men still died: Alejandro Hernández Fuentes, Dorlian Ronial Castillo Cabrera, Maynor Yassir Suazo Sandoval, Miguel Ángel Luna González, and two of their co-workers. A cable-stayed replacement is expected to open in late 2030, at an estimated 4.3 to 5.2 billion dollars. At 1:25 that morning, a breaker opened because one wire had let go. It had waited nine years. The bridge had waited longer. As Homendy put it, like all of the accidents we investigate, this was preventable.

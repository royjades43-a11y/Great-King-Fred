It's late. You're lying in bed, scrolling. And there it is. Their name. You open the chat. You check when they were last online. Three minutes ago. You type, "Hey, stranger." You know how this ended last time. You remember the fights, the silence, the night one of you finally said, "I'm done." But for you, it doesn't feel like going back. It feels like going home. If that's you, you're far from alone. Breaking up and making up is much more common than most people think. And for some of us, it happens again and again. Sound familiar? Psychologists have scanned the brains of people who just got dumped. They've followed couples who break up and make up. And they've studied what happens when you keep checking your ex's profile. By the end of this video, you'll know why your ex is so hard to quit. And the one question that tells a second chance from a second mistake. Let's start with what a breakup does to your brain.

In twenty eleven, psychologist Ethan Kross and his team recruited forty people. All of them had been through an unwanted breakup in the last six months. Inside a brain scanner, they looked at a photo of their ex and thought about the breakup. In a separate task, the researchers pressed a hot probe against their forearm. Painful, but bearable. Then they compared the two. Some of the same areas lit up for both. Areas linked to the physical feeling of pain. So when you say a breakup hurts, it may be more than a figure of speech. A year earlier, anthropologist Helen Fisher and her team scanned fifteen people who'd just been dumped. They were still intensely in love. On average, the breakup was about two months old. When they saw their ex's photo, the brain's reward system lit up. Including areas linked to craving and addiction. The researchers described romantic rejection as a kind of addiction. And here's the irony. Being rejected didn't make them want their ex less. In many ways, it made them want them more. Fisher called it frustration attraction. The person who caused the pain becomes the one thing your brain wants to stop it. So is it any wonder you reach for your phone at one in the morning? But there was good news hidden in that study too. The longer it had been since the breakup, the weaker the activity in an area linked to attachment. The craving fades. It just doesn't fade on your schedule.

So the pain is real. But why does going back feel like such a good idea? Part of the answer is your memory. Psychologists have found that the feelings attached to bad memories usually fade faster than the feelings attached to good ones. It's called the fading affect bias. Most of the time, that's a gift. It's how we move on from bad days. But after a breakup, it plays a trick on you. Your mind starts cutting a highlight reel. The road trip. The inside jokes. The way they said your name. Not the fight in the car on that same road trip. You might even miss the things you used to complain about. The snoring. The terrible playlist. You remember why you loved them. You forget why you left. Then loneliness adds its own voice. Psychologist Stephanie Spielmann and her colleagues studied people's longing for their exes. People with a stronger fear of being single longed for their ex more. They were also more likely to try to get back together. It didn't matter who ended it. And there's one more pull. Communication researcher René Dailey has studied on-again, off-again couples for years. One of the most common reasons people give for going back is simple. Lingering feelings. Another is familiarity. Starting over with someone new is hard work. Your ex already knows your coffee order. So here's a question worth asking yourself. Do you miss them, or do you miss being known?

Now, maybe you haven't texted them. But be honest. Do you check their profile? In twenty twelve, psychologist Tara Marshall surveyed four hundred and sixty-four people about their exes on Facebook. The more people checked their ex's page, the worse they were doing. More distress about the breakup. More longing for their ex. And less personal growth. Seeing an ex in person had a similar link. More contact, more longing. Now, these were correlations. People who are struggling may simply check more. But it's hard to heal a wound you reopen every night. Every scroll through their photos adds another clip to the highlight reel.

So what happens when people do go back? In twenty thirteen, a team led by sociologist Sarah Halpern-Meekin studied almost eight hundred young adults. More than four in ten had broken up and got back together at least once. The researchers called it relationship churning. Think about your own friends. You can probably name a couple like that. And it doesn't stop at dating. In twenty fourteen, Amber Vennum and her colleagues studied more than a thousand couples who lived together or were married. Over a third of the couples living together had broken up and made up. So had about one in five married couples. Couples who'd cycled before the wedding started their marriages less satisfied. They argued more. And they were more likely to have a trial separation in the first three years. Couples who'd cycled once were also more likely to cycle again. In twenty eighteen, a study led by Kale Monk linked more on-off cycling to more psychological distress. So the pattern can wear you down. Even when each reunion feels like coming home. But it wasn't over yet.

Because not every couple who goes back ends up where they started. On average, on-again, off-again couples report lower satisfaction and more uncertainty. But some of them do well. The difference often comes down to one thing. Whether anything actually changed. Some couples split over distance, bad timing or a job in another city. If that problem is gone, going back can make sense. Researchers who study couples talk about sliding versus deciding. Sliding is drifting back because it's late, you're lonely, and they texted first. Deciding is choosing it with your eyes open. So be honest. Are you deciding right now, or sliding? Vennum's team found that cycling couples tended to rush into big steps, like moving in together. So here's the one question I promised you. What's different this time? Not how you feel. What's different. If the honest answer is "I missed them," that's the highlight reel talking. If you can both name what changed, and you can see it, that's a second chance. Real change looks like a hard conversation about why it ended. New habits, not new promises. Time apart that you both actually used. So how do you know which one you're in?

Rule one: ask the question. Before you reply, write down what's different this time. If you can't fill one line, don't send the text.

Rule two: watch the whole movie. Write down why it ended. The real reasons, not the polished version. Read it when the highlight reel starts playing.

Rule three: mute, don't monitor. You don't have to block them. But stop checking. Mute them, so their life isn't the first thing you see each morning. Every look reopens the wound.

Rule four: ride out the craving. Remember the brain scans. The pull gets weaker with time. Cravings come in waves. Most waves pass. When the urge hits, wait twenty minutes. Call a friend instead.

And rule five: fill the space they left. Your friends, your plans, the parts of you that went quiet. In one study, psychologists Ty Tashiro and Patricia Frazier found that growth after a breakup was common. Many people said they'd become more confident and more independent.

So here's the twist. Missing your ex doesn't mean you should go back. It means you loved someone. And your brain is doing exactly what brains do after a loss. People who keep going back usually aren't weak. Very often, they're loyal. They hold on, because holding on is what they do best. Loyalty is a good thing. Pointed at the wrong person, it keeps you stuck. Just make sure you're holding on to a person, not a highlight reel. The next time their name lights up your phone, remember the whole movie. Then ask what's different this time. If nothing is, you already have your answer.

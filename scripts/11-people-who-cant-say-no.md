# Video 11: The Psychology of People Who Can't Say No

**Title:** The Psychology of People Who Can't Say No
**Length:** 1,537 words. That's about 8:40 at the pace of video 10's voice, or 9:30 at the older voices' pace.
Either way it's over 8:00, so mid-roll ads are possible.

**How it differs from competitors:** most videos on this topic are "10 signs you're a people pleaser" or
"just set boundaries". This one shows what researchers measured: strangers writing "pickle" in a library
book, a copy-machine reason that wasn't a reason, how we misjudge next month's free time, and who gets asked
to do the office chores. It lands kindly. People who can't say no are often the kindest in the room, but a
yes only means something if a no was possible.

**Engagement design:**
- **Talks straight to the viewer throughout:** "but for you", "think about your own workplace", "how many
  things on your calendar did past you agree to?".
- **Opens on the viewer's own moment:** a tired Thursday night and a "Sure! What time?" they regret instantly.
- **An open loop paid off later:** "the one small word swap" is answered at about 6:40 ("I don't", not "I can't"),
  then again in rule three.
- **A running image:** the Saturday. It starts in the hook, comes back in "even when the request is just a
  Saturday" and "the same Saturday you wanted to yourself", and closes the video with the phone buzzing again.
- **Irony and human detail:**
  - the reason that wasn't a reason ("because I have to make copies")
  - the word "pickle"
  - "future you is just you"
  - "the reward for helping is more requests"
- **A rhetorical question about every 90 seconds**, and "But it wasn't over yet." at the end of section 5.

Lines in *[brackets]* are visual notes for the edit; they are not read out. Numbers and years are written the

## [0:00–0:55] HOOK

It's Thursday night. You're finally on the couch. Your phone buzzes.
*[a stick figure sinks into the couch; a phone buzzes on the cushion]*
"Hey! Any chance you could help me move on Saturday?"
*[a chat bubble pops up; a small stack of moving boxes appears above it]*
You were going to sleep in. You were going to do nothing at all.
*[a thought cloud: a bed, a little "zzz"]*
You type, "Sure! What time?"
And the second you hit send, a tiny voice says, "Why did I just do that?"
*[the bubble sends with a whoosh; the figure freezes, mouth open; the thought cloud pops]*
Sound familiar?
You're not lazy, and you're not selfish. But for you, no doesn't feel like a small word. It feels like a fight you're starting.
*[top: "for **you**, no feels like a **fight**"; a tiny "NO" in a speech bubble, with a lightning bolt beside it]*
Psychologists have studied why no is so hard to say. They've asked strangers to write in a library book. They've
tested which refusals actually work. And they've found out who gets asked the most.
*[three icons line up as they're named: a book with a pen, a speech bubble, a hand going up]*
By the end of this video, you'll know why yes slips out before you can stop it. And the one small word swap
that makes no much easier.
Let's start with why no feels so dangerous.

## [0:55–2:25] WHY NO FEELS DANGEROUS

For most of human history, being pushed out of the group was dangerous. Alone, you were far less likely to survive.
*[a circle of stick figures; one steps outside it and the background around it dims]*
In 1995, Roy Baumeister and Mark Leary called this the need to belong. They argued it's one of our most basic human drives.
So when someone asks you for something, part of your brain hears a different question. Do you still want to be part of the group?
*[the asker's bubble says "help me move?"; a second, ghostly bubble behind it reads "still one of us?"]*
No can feel like the wrong answer. Even when the request is just a Saturday.

But here's the thing. The fear is usually much bigger than the fallout.
In 2023, Julian Givi and Colleen Kirk ran five experiments with more than 2,000 people.
Some imagined being invited to something. A museum exhibit. A dinner with a celebrity chef. Others imagined
being the one who sent the invitation.
*[two figures: an inviter holding an envelope, an invitee; a picture frame, then a chef's hat]*
The people saying no expected the worst: angry, hurt, no more invitations.
*[the invitee's thought cloud: a storm, a broken heart, a torn envelope]*
The people who got the no? Mostly, they were fine.
*[the inviter shrugs and smiles; a tiny "ok!" bubble]*
Here's the irony. We all know how little a no bothers us when we're the one asking. We just forget it the
moment we're the one saying it.
After people got turned down themselves, they worried less about saying no to others.
So why does yes keep slipping out anyway?

## [2:25–4:05] YES ON AUTOPILOT

Most yeses aren't decisions. They're reflexes.
In the late 1970s, Ellen Langer and her colleagues ran a sneaky experiment at a university copy machine.
*[a big boxy copier; a figure at it; a second figure walks up]*
"Excuse me, I have five pages. May I use the copy machine?" About 60% let them.
"Because I'm in a rush." About 94% said yes.
"May I use the copy machine, because I have to make copies?" About 93%.
*[three bars grow as each version is read: 60, 94, 93; on the third, a small "…" bubble over the person at the machine]*
Of course they had to make copies. Everyone at the machine had to make copies.
The word because did most of the work. Your brain heard the shape of a reason, and it said yes on autopilot.
*[top: "**because**…"; the word "because" glows red on the bubble]*

In 2014, Vanessa Bohns and her colleagues sent students out with an odd request. They asked strangers to
write one word in pen in a library book. The word was pickle.
*[an open book; a pen writes "pickle" in it, letter by letter]*
The students guessed about 28% would agree. In fact, more than 64% wrote pickle in the book.
*[two bars: "guessed 28%", "did it 64%"]*
Many of them hesitated. Some even called it vandalism. And then they did it anyway.
Because saying no to someone's face is awkward. And for a lot of us, awkward feels worse than wrong.
So think about the last time you said yes. Did you decide, or did your mouth decide for you?

## [4:05–5:05] THE FUTURE-YOU TRAP

In 2005, Gal Zauberman and John Lynch asked people how much spare time they'd have today, and a month from now.
Most expected more free time in the future. They were more optimistic about future time than about future money.
*[a calendar: today is crammed with blocks; next month looks wide open]*
But next month always arrives just as busy as this one.
*[the calendar flips; next month fills up just as full]*
So a favor in five weeks feels free. It's a problem for future you.
Then future you shows up, and future you is just you. Same job. Same tiredness. Same Saturday you wanted to yourself.
*[two identical stick figures face each other, "now" and "future", both yawning]*
It's been nicknamed the "Yes, damn" effect. You say yes. Then the day comes, and you say damn.
*[top: "yes… *damn*"]*
How many things on your calendar right now did past you agree to?

## [5:05–6:15] WHO GETS ASKED

The people who say yes the most get asked the most.
In 2017, Linda Babcock and her colleagues studied tasks nobody wants: the ones that need doing, but never get
you promoted. Taking notes. Planning the office party. Sitting on one more committee.
*[a clipboard, a party hat, a row of chairs]*
In their experiments, women volunteered for these tasks more than men.
When asked directly, women said yes about 76% of the time. Men, about 51%.
*[two bars: 76 and 51]*
Both men and women were more likely to ask a woman. People asked the person they expected to say yes.
*[a group of figures; several arrows all point at the same figure]*
Think about your own workplace. Your own family. Your own group chat. Who always gets asked to organize, cover, or fix it?
If it's you, it may be because everyone knows you won't say no.
That's the irony of being reliable. The reward for helping is more requests.
*[a stack of requests piles up on the helpful figure's arms]*
But it wasn't over yet.

## [6:15–7:30] WHAT YES COSTS, AND THE WORD THAT HELPS

In 2005, Mark Bolino and William Turnley studied employees who regularly went above and beyond. Going the
extra mile was linked to more overload, more job stress, and more conflict between work and home.
*[a figure at a desk under a growing pile; a little house in the corner with a clock]*
Helping isn't the problem. The problem is the yes you didn't choose.
Every yes to something you don't want is a no to something you do. Your sleep. Your family. The project you
keep pushing to next month.
*[a balance: "yes" on one side; sleep, a house and a notebook slide off the other]*

In 2012, Vanessa Patrick and Henrik Hagtvedt tested two tiny phrases: "I can't" and "I don't."
People practiced one, then picked a thank-you snack: a chocolate bar or a granola bar.
About 64% of the "I don't" group picked the granola bar, against about 39% of the "I can't" group.
*[two bars: "I don't" 64, "I can't" 39; a granola bar icon above them]*
"I can't" sounds like a rule someone gave you, so it invites a negotiation. "Can't you just this once?"
"I don't" sounds like who you are. In a second paper, "I don't" refusals persuaded other people too.
They signaled conviction. People don't usually argue with who you are.
*[top: "I *don't*"; the figure stands taller]*

## [7:30–8:35] FIVE RULES

1. **Buy yourself time.** "Let me check and get back to you." *[an hourglass]*
2. **Make it tomorrow.** Would I say yes if it were tomorrow? *[the calendar page flips to "tomorrow"]*
3. **Say "I don't," not "I can't."** *[the "can't" crossed out]*
4. **Keep it short and kind.** "Thank you for asking. I'm going to pass this time." *[a long scroll shrinks to a short note]*
5. **Remember the other side.** Would you stop being their friend? *[the two figures swap places]*

## [8:35–9:15] THE TWIST AND THE ENDING

People who can't say no aren't weak. Very often, they're the kindest people in the room.
But a yes only means something if a no was possible.
*[a gift box; when it's handed over too many times it turns grey, then red again on a chosen yes]*
The next time your phone buzzes, take a breath. Ask yourself if it were tomorrow. And if the honest answer is
no, say it kindly, and let it go.
*[the couch from the opening; the phone buzzes; the figure smiles and types "I'm going to pass this time 💛"]*
Challenge: say one small no this week, and come back and tell me if anyone even minded.

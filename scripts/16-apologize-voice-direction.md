A line-by-line reading guide, in the same format as videos 5 to 15 (see `scripts/05-always-late-voice-direction.md`
for the 12 rules and the full legend). It works for a human narrator, ElevenLabs or AI Studio (use each
section's **Direction** line as the style prompt).

**This is the first script under the storytelling rule (owner's rule, Nov 2026):**
- No years and no researcher names in the voiceover. Each study is told as a scene the viewer walks through, and the

- Like video 13, it's written to be spoken, one friend to another.

Numbers are written the way they are spoken.

## 1 · Hook (0:00–1:15)
**Direction:** light and knowing, like you've both done it this week. The chair line gets a smile. The promise is warm and clear, straight to the viewer. Energy 3, rising to 4.

Someone bumps into you in the street. And somehow, you're the one who says sorry. ||
You say sorry to the waiter when your food is wrong. ||
You say sorry when someone else steps on your foot. ||
You say sorry before you ask a question. ||
And once, maybe, you said sorry to a chair. ||
Some of us say it so often, we don't even hear ourselves anymore. ||
If that sounds like you, you're in very good company. ||
So in this video, we're going to look at why some of us say sorry for almost everything. ||
You'll find out about a strange little apology that made strangers hand over their phones. ||
And by the end, you'll know what makes an apology actually work, and when to stop saying it. ||
Let's start with a simple question. Who says sorry the most? ||
A pair of researchers in Canada asked people to keep a diary for twelve days. ||
Every evening, they wrote down every time they had apologized that day. ||
They also wrote down every time they thought they had done something wrong. ||
Small things counted too. Bumping into someone. Running a few minutes late. Interrupting a friend. ||
Women reported apologizing more often than men. ||
But here's the part most people miss. ||
Men apologized just as often as women, for the things they thought were wrong. ||
They just thought fewer things needed an apology in the first place. ||
Then they asked people to imagine small moments, like being a little rude to a friend by accident. ||
The men tended to rate those moments as less serious. ||
So the difference came down to where each person draws the line. ||
Nobody here is doing it wrong. We just set the bar in different places. ||
Have you ever wondered where your own line sits? ||

## 2 · The rain (1:15–2:30)
**Direction:** a little street story, told with a smile. Paint the scene: the rain, the station, the stranger. A beat before the numbers. Energy 4.

Here's where it gets interesting. ||
Picture a busy train station on a rainy day. ||
A young man walks up to strangers, one by one, and asks to borrow their phone. ||
That's a big ask. Most people would say no, and most people did. ||
Only about nine people out of every hundred handed it over. ||
Then he changed one small thing. ||
Before asking, he said, "I'm so sorry about the rain." ||
He obviously didn't cause the rain. ||
The rain had nothing to do with him. That's what makes it so strange. ||
But suddenly, almost half of the strangers lent him their phone. ||
That's around five times as many, from five extra words. ||
The researchers think that little apology showed he cared about how the other person was feeling. ||
And people trust someone who seems to care. ||
The same team found that people who made these small, extra apologies seemed warmer and easier to trust. ||
So the word "sorry" can do a lot more than admit guilt. ||
Very often, it's a small way of saying, "I see you." ||

## 3 · How sorry calms anger (2:30–3:20)
**Direction:** clear and gentle, like explaining why something works. "Less likely to get back at them" lands with a small smile. Energy 3.

That's also why a real apology can calm someone down so quickly. ||
In one experiment, a research assistant made a careless mistake that made people look bad on a task. ||
Some people got an apology. Others got nothing. ||
The people who heard "sorry" felt less angry. ||
They liked the assistant more. ||
And when they had a chance to get back at the assistant, they were much less likely to take it. ||
So for most of us, saying sorry is a kind of social glue. ||
Think about the last time someone cut in front of you in a queue. ||
Then they turned and said, "Oh, sorry, I didn't see you." ||
Most of the annoyance just melts away. ||
It keeps small bumps from turning into big fights. ||

## 4 · Refusing to apologize (3:20–4:20)
**Direction:** curious and fair, never judgy. The twist is "it feels good to refuse, for a while." Slow down for the question at the end: it's the re-hook. Energy 3, rising.

But then there are people who almost never apologize. ||
You probably know at least one. ||
The person who would rather argue for an hour than say two small words. ||
So a team of psychologists asked people to remember a time they had hurt someone. ||
Some of them wrote an apology. Others wrote a message refusing to apologize. ||
And the people who refused actually felt more powerful afterwards. ||
For a while, they even felt a little better about themselves. ||
Refusing to say sorry can feel like protecting yourself. ||
It feels strong in the moment. ||
But that good feeling doesn't fix anything for the other person. ||
And it can quietly cost you the relationship. ||
And most of us find it much easier to trust someone who can admit a mistake. ||
So if apologies are this powerful, why do so many of them still fall flat? ||

## 5 · What makes an apology work (4:20–5:35)
**Direction:** the payoff, clear and warm. Count the parts gently. "Taking responsibility" is the headline, so land it. Then a real pause before "But it wasn't over yet.", low and close. Energy 4.

A group of researchers wanted to find out which parts of an apology matter most. ||
They broke a good apology into six parts. ||
Saying you're sorry. Explaining what happened. Admitting it was your fault. ||
Saying it won't happen again. Offering to fix it. And asking to be forgiven. ||
Then they asked hundreds of people to rate apologies with different parts missing. ||
The most important part, by far, was admitting it was your fault. ||
Not "I'm sorry you feel that way." Just, "I got that wrong." ||
The second most important part was offering to fix it. ||
And the part that mattered least? Asking to be forgiven. ||
So the strongest apologies are about the other person, not about getting yourself off the hook. ||
Imagine you forgot a friend's birthday. ||
"Sorry, I've just been so busy," is a weak apology. ||
"I forgot your birthday, and that's on me. Can I take you to dinner this week?" is a strong one. ||
But it wasn't over yet. ||

## 6 · Timing (5:35–6:30)
**Direction:** curious, like sharing a secret that changes how you'll apologize tonight. Warm. Energy 4.

Because when you apologize matters too. ||
Researchers asked people to think about a time someone had upset them. ||
Some imagined getting an apology straight away. Others imagined getting it later, after they had a chance to talk. ||
The later apologies landed better. ||
Waiting on its own didn't do the trick. ||
What made the difference was that, by then, the person felt properly heard. ||
A rushed "sorry" can feel like a way to close the conversation. ||
Think about how it feels when someone apologizes while you're still in the middle of explaining. ||
It can make you more annoyed, not less. ||
A sorry that comes after listening feels like you actually understood. ||
So the next time you need to apologize, try listening first. ||
One honest sentence after listening is worth more than ten quick sorries. ||

## 7 · Sorry to yourself (6:30–7:20)
**Direction:** gentle and close, almost one-to-one. This is for the over-apologizers. Energy 3.

There's one more apology most of us forget. The one we owe ourselves. ||
In another study, people thought about something they regretted doing. ||
Some were asked to write to themselves kindly, the way a good friend would. ||
Those people were more motivated to make amends, and to avoid doing it again. ||
They weren't letting themselves off the hook. They were giving themselves the energy to do better. ||
Being harsh with yourself can feel responsible. ||
If you're someone who says sorry for everything, you're probably also very good at being hard on yourself. ||
But kindness seems to make it easier to actually do better. ||
Next time you replay a mistake at night, talk to yourself like a friend who made it. ||

## 8 · Five rules (7:20–8:20)
**Direction:** friendly and practical, like tips from a friend. Each "Rule" is a small headline. Energy 3.

So how can you use this in your own life? Here are five simple rules. ||
Rule one. Save "sorry" for when you've actually done something. ||
If you're just asking a question, try "excuse me," or simply ask it. ||
Rule two. Try swapping sorry for thank you. ||
Instead of "Sorry I'm late," try "Thank you for waiting." ||
It still shows you care, without making you smaller. ||
It puts the focus on their kindness, instead of on your mistake. ||
Rule three. When you do apologize, say what you did, and how you'll fix it. ||
Rule four. Let the other person speak first. Then apologize. ||
Ask them how it felt for them, and really listen to the answer. ||
Rule five. After you've apologized, forgive yourself too. ||
You can't take it back. You can only do better next time. ||

## 9 · Twist and ending (8:20–9:15)
**Direction:** soft and generous, talking to one person. The chair callback is warm and a little funny. The challenge is bright. Energy 2.

So here's the twist. ||
People who say sorry a lot are usually people who care a lot about others. ||
That's a good thing. The goal is to make your sorry mean something again. ||
But say sorry forty times a day, and the one that really counts gets lost in the crowd. ||
Save it for the moments that matter, and say it properly when they come. ||
And the next time you bump into a chair, you have my permission to say nothing at all. ||
Here's your challenge for this week. ||
Every time you're about to say "sorry" for something small, swap it for "thank you." ||
Then notice how people react, and how you feel. ||
Most people won't even notice the swap. But you will. ||
And I'd love to know something. What's the strangest thing you've ever apologized to? ||
Let me know in the comments. ||
Which habit should we cover next? Drop it in the comments. ||

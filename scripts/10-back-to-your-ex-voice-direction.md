# Voice direction: "The Psychology of People Who Keep Going Back to Their Ex"

A line-by-line reading guide, in the same format as videos 5 to 9 (see `scripts/05-always-late-voice-direction.md`
for the 12 rules and the full legend). It works for a human narrator, ElevenLabs or AI Studio (use each
section's **Direction** line as the style prompt), and for our free AI voice through `voice/directed.py`.

| Mark | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **word** | stress it |
| `\|` / `\|\|` | short / long beat |
| ↘ / ↗ | land down / lift (a genuine question) |
| (brisk) (slower) (land it) (lean in) (warm) (smile) (light) (character) | how to deliver the line |

Numbers are written the way they are spoken.

## Energy map

```
energy  5 |
        4 |  *                                     *  *
        3 |     *  *     *   *        *  *    *          *
        2 |  *        *         *  *        *               *  *
        1 |
          +----------------------------------------------------
            hook  brain   reel  checking  going back  works rules twist end
```

The hook is quiet and intimate: late at night, one person and a phone. The brain section is a curious
storyteller, a little awed at the irony. The highlight reel is warm and knowing, with a smile on the
snoring and the coffee order. The checking section is honest and gentle, never scolding. The numbers are
calm and fair. "When going back works" is hopeful but clear-eyed. The rules are bright and practical, and the
twist is soft and kind.

This script talks straight to the viewer ("for you", "be honest", "your ex already knows your coffee
order"). Read those asides as if to one friend at night, a little quieter and closer than the lines around them.

---

## 1 · Hook (0:00–1:00)
**Direction:** hushed and intimate, as if it's one in the morning. Short beats between the opening lines.
"Hey, stranger" is said the way the viewer would type it, a little hopeful. "But for you" is personal and close.
A real smile on "Sound familiar?". Energy 2, rising to 4 by the promise.

(slower) It's **late**. | You're lying in bed, | **scrolling**. ||
(lean in) And there it **is**. | Their **name**. ||
You open the **chat**. | (character) You type, | "Hey, **stranger**." ||
You know how this ended **last** time. |
You remember the **fights**, | the **silence**, | the night one of you finally said, | (character) "I'm **done**." ||
(lean in) But for **you**, | it doesn't feel like going **back**. | (warm) It feels like going **home**. ||
(warm) If that's you, | you're far from **alone**. |
Breaking up and making up | is much more **common** than most people think. |
And for some of us, | it happens again and **again**. ||
(smile) Sound **familiar**? ↗ ||
(brisk) Psychologists have scanned the brains | of people who just got **dumped**. |
They've followed couples | who break up and make **up**. |
And they've studied what happens | when you keep checking your ex's **profile**. ||
By the end of this video, | you'll know why your ex | is so hard to **quit**. |
(lean in) And the **one** question | that tells a second **chance** | from a second **mistake**. ||
Let's start with what a breakup does | to your **brain**. ||

## 2 · Your brain on heartbreak (1:00–2:35)
**Direction:** a curious storyteller. Clear and steady on the studies; act "Painful, but bearable" lightly.
The irony is the heart of the section: slow down on "made them want them more" and land "the one thing your
brain wants to stop it". The good news at the end is warm. Energy 3.

(brisk) In twenty eleven, | psychologist Ethan **Kross** and his team | recruited **forty** people. |
All of them had been through an **unwanted** breakup | in the last six **months**. ||
Inside a brain scanner, | they looked at a photo of their **ex** | and thought about the breakup. |
In a separate task, | the researchers pressed a hot **probe** | against their forearm. |
(light) Painful, | but **bearable**. ||
Then they compared the **two**. |
(lean in) Some of the **same** areas | lit up for **both**. |
Areas linked to the **physical** feeling of pain. ||
(land it) So when you say a breakup **hurts**, | it may be more than a figure of **speech**. ||
(brisk) A year earlier, | anthropologist Helen **Fisher** and her team | scanned **fifteen** people | who'd just been dumped. |
They were still **intensely** in love. |
On average, | the breakup was about two months **old**. |
When they saw their ex's photo, | the brain's **reward** system lit up. |
Including areas linked to **craving** | and **addiction**. |
The researchers described romantic rejection | as a kind of **addiction**. ||
(lean in) And here's the **irony**. |
Being rejected didn't make them want their ex **less**. |
(slower) In many ways, | it made them want them **more**. ||
Fisher called it | **frustration** attraction. |
(land it) The person who caused the pain | becomes the one thing your brain wants | to **stop** it. ||
(smile) So is it any wonder | you reach for your phone at **one** in the morning? ↗ ||
(warm) But there was good news | hidden in that study **too**. |
The **longer** it had been since the breakup, | the **weaker** the activity | in an area linked to attachment. |
(warm) The craving **fades**. | (smile) It just doesn't fade on **your** schedule. ||

## 3 · The highlight reel (2:35–4:10)
**Direction:** warm and knowing. The memory idea is explained simply. The highlight reel is a list read
with affection; "Not the fight in the car" is the turn. Smile on "The snoring. The terrible playlist." and on
the coffee order. The closing question is real and gentle. Energy 3.

(land it) So the pain is **real**. |
(lean in) But why does going back | feel like such a **good** idea? ↗ ||
Part of the answer | is your **memory**. |
Psychologists have found | that the feelings attached to **bad** memories | usually fade **faster** | than the feelings attached to **good** ones. |
It's called | the fading affect **bias**. ||
(warm) Most of the time, | that's a **gift**. | It's how we move on from bad **days**. |
(lean in) But after a breakup, | it plays a **trick** on you. ||
Your mind starts cutting | a **highlight** reel. ||
(warm) The road **trip**. | The inside **jokes**. | The way they said your **name**. ||
(land it) **Not** the fight in the car | on that same road trip. ||
(smile) You might even miss the things | you used to **complain** about. |
(light) The **snoring**. | The terrible **playlist**. ||
You remember why you **loved** them. | (slower) You forget why you **left**. ||
Then loneliness adds its own **voice**. |
Psychologist Stephanie **Spielmann** and her colleagues | studied people's longing for their exes. |
People with a stronger fear of being **single** | longed for their ex **more**. |
They were also more likely | to try to get back **together**. |
It didn't matter | who **ended** it. ||
(lean in) And there's one more **pull**. |
Communication researcher René **Dailey** | has studied on-again, off-again couples for **years**. |
One of the most common reasons people give for going back | is **simple**. |
Lingering **feelings**. | Another is **familiarity**. |
Starting over with someone new | is hard **work**. |
(smile) Your ex already knows | your **coffee** order. ||
(warm) So here's a question | worth asking yourself. |
(slower) Do you miss **them**, | or do you miss being **known**? ↗ ||

## 4 · The checking (4:10–4:50)
**Direction:** honest and kind, never scolding. "But be honest" is a small smile. The three results are
read plainly, one beat each. Be fair on "these were correlations"; land the wound line softly. Energy 2–3.

(light) Now, | maybe you haven't **texted** them. |
(smile) But be **honest**. | Do you check their **profile**? ↗ ||
In twenty twelve, | psychologist Tara **Marshall** surveyed | four hundred and sixty-four people | about their exes on **Facebook**. |
The **more** people checked their ex's page, | the **worse** they were doing. |
(brisk) More **distress** about the breakup. | More **longing** for their ex. | And **less** personal growth. ||
Seeing an ex in **person** | had a similar link. |
More **contact**, | more **longing**. ||
(light) Now, | these were **correlations**. |
People who are struggling | may simply check **more**. |
(warm) But it's hard to heal a wound | you **reopen** every night. |
Every scroll through their photos | adds another clip | to the **highlight** reel. ||

## 5 · What happens when people go back (4:50–5:55)
**Direction:** calm and fair, like laying the facts on the table. Clear on the numbers. "They argued more"
is a short, plain beat. Slow down on the last two lines, and give "But it wasn't over yet." a real lift.
Energy 2–3.

(lean in) So what happens | when people **do** go back? ↗ ||
In twenty thirteen, | a team led by sociologist Sarah **Halpern-Meekin** | studied almost eight **hundred** young adults. |
More than **four** in ten | had broken up and got back together | at least **once**. |
The researchers called it | relationship **churning**. ||
And it doesn't stop at **dating**. |
In twenty fourteen, | Amber **Vennum** and her colleagues | studied more than a **thousand** couples | who lived together or were married. |
Over a **third** of the couples living together | had broken up and made up. |
So had about **one** in five | **married** couples. ||
Couples who'd cycled before the wedding | started their marriages **less** satisfied. |
They argued **more**. |
And they were more likely to have a trial **separation** | in the first three years. ||
Couples who'd cycled **once** | were also more likely to cycle **again**. |
In twenty eighteen, | a study led by Kale **Monk** | linked more on-off cycling | to more psychological **distress**. ||
(slower) So the pattern | can wear you **down**. |
Even when each reunion | feels like coming **home**. ||
(lean in) But it wasn't over **yet**. ||

## 6 · When going back works (5:55–7:15)
**Direction:** hopeful but clear-eyed. Lift on "But some of them do well." Act the sliding line lightly
(it's late, you're lonely). The promised question is the centre of the video: slow down and give it room.
"Not how you feel. What's different." is two firm beats. Energy 3, rising to 4.

(warm) Because not every couple who goes back | ends up where they **started**. |
On average, | on-again, off-again couples | report lower **satisfaction** | and more **uncertainty**. |
(lean in) But some of them | do **well**. ||
The difference often comes down to | **one** thing. |
(land it) Whether anything actually **changed**. ||
Some couples split over **distance**, | bad **timing** | or a job in another **city**. |
If that problem is **gone**, | going back can make **sense**. ||
Researchers who study couples | talk about **sliding** | versus **deciding**. |
(light) Sliding is drifting back | because it's **late**, | you're **lonely**, | and they texted **first**. |
(land it) Deciding is choosing it | with your eyes **open**. ||
Vennum's team found | that cycling couples tended to **rush** into big steps, | like moving in **together**. ||
(lean in) So here's the **one** question | I promised you. ||
(slower) What's **different** | this time? ↗ ||
Not how you **feel**. | (land it) What's **different**. ||
If the honest answer is | (character) "I **missed** them," | that's the highlight reel **talking**. |
(warm) If you can both name what **changed**, | and you can **see** it, | that's a second **chance**. ||
Real change looks like | a **hard** conversation | about why it ended. |
New **habits**, | not new **promises**. |
Time apart | that you both actually **used**. ||
(brisk) So how do you know | which one you're **in**? ↗ ||

## 7 · Five rules (7:15–8:15)
**Direction:** a bright, practical coach. Each "Rule…" is a clean new step with a smile. The rule names are
said like titles. Energy 4.

(brisk) Rule **one**: | ask the **question**. |
Before you reply, | write down what's **different** this time. |
(land it) If you can't fill **one** line, | don't send the **text**. ||

(brisk) Rule **two**: | watch the **whole** movie. |
Write down why it **ended**. |
The **real** reasons, | not the polished version. |
Read it when the highlight reel | starts **playing**. ||

(brisk) Rule **three**: | **mute**, | don't **monitor**. |
You don't have to **block** them. | But stop **checking**. |
Mute them, | so their life isn't the **first** thing you see | each morning. |
Every look | **reopens** the wound. ||

(brisk) Rule **four**: | ride out the **craving**. |
Remember the brain **scans**. | The pull gets **weaker** with time. |
When the urge hits, | wait twenty **minutes**. |
(warm) Call a **friend** instead. ||

(brisk) And rule **five**: | fill the **space** they left. |
(warm) Your **friends**, | your **plans**, | the parts of you that went **quiet**. |
In one study, | psychologists Ty **Tashiro** and Patricia **Frazier** | found that growth after a breakup | was **common**. |
Many people said they'd become | more **confident** | and more **independent**. ||

## 8 · The twist and the ending (8:15–8:50)
**Direction:** the softest part. Slow and sure on the twist. "Very often, they're loyal." is warm and
generous. The last line before the challenge lands gently, not as a verdict. The challenge and CTA are
friendly and upbeat. Energy 2, then 4 for the challenge.

(slower) So here's the **twist**. ||
Missing your ex | doesn't mean you should go **back**. |
(warm) It means you **loved** someone. |
And your brain is doing **exactly** what brains do | after a **loss**. ||
People who keep going back | usually aren't **weak**. |
(warm) Very often, | they're **loyal**. |
They hold **on**, | because holding on | is what they do **best**. ||
(lean in) Just make sure you're holding on to a **person**, | not a highlight **reel**. ||
The next time their name | lights up your **phone**, | remember the **whole** movie. |
Then ask what's **different** this time. |
(slower) If nothing **is**, | you already have your **answer**. ||

(brisk) So here's your **challenge** for this week. |
Write down **three** real reasons it ended. |
Keep the list somewhere you'll **see** it. |
Then come back and tell me if it **helped**. ||

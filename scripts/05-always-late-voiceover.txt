"I'll be there in five minutes." You've sent that text. Maybe today. And twenty minutes later, someone is still sitting alone at a table, watching the door. Here's the strange part. You weren't lying when you sent it. You really believed five minutes. So did a Nobel Prize winner. So did an entire country, for ten years. By the end of this video, you'll know why your brain gets time wrong. And the one question that fixes it. Let's find out where those missing fifteen minutes actually go.

Let's start with the country. In 1957, Sydney picked a design for a new opera house. The plan said it would be finished by 1963, for about seven million dollars. It opened in 1973. Ten years late. And the final bill was about one hundred and two million dollars. That's more than fourteen times the original price. The architect, Jørn Utzon, never even saw it finished. He quit in 1966, after a fight over money, and went home to Denmark. When the Queen opened his building in 1973, he reportedly wasn't even watching on TV. You'd think that kind of disaster only happens to giant projects. It doesn't. Because the man who explained it was making the same mistake at the very same time.

His name was Daniel Kahneman. In the early 1970s, he was on a team writing a new school textbook in Israel. After a year of work, he asked everyone a simple question. How long until we're done? Everyone wrote down a number. They all said about two years. Then Kahneman turned to the team's curriculum expert and asked something different. How long did other teams like ours take? The expert went quiet. Then he admitted it. About forty percent of those teams never finished at all. The rest took seven to ten years. So how did their own team compare? "Below average," he said. "But not by much." Nobody quit. Nobody changed the plan. The book took eight years. Kahneman later wrote that they should probably have quit that day. In 1979, Kahneman and Amos Tversky gave this mistake its name: the planning fallacy. We plan for the best-case future. Then the real one shows up. That same year, the writer Douglas Hofstadter published a joke about it. "It always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account Hofstadter's Law." It's a joke. It's also painfully accurate. And the numbers back it up.

The economist Bent Flyvbjerg collected data on more than sixteen thousand big projects, in over a hundred countries. Only about eight and a half percent finished on time and on budget. Just half a percent also delivered everything they promised. He calls it the iron law: over budget, over time, over and over again. Boston's "Big Dig" tunnel was first budgeted at about two point eight billion dollars. It finished years late, in 2007, at about fourteen point six billion. Even airlines plan around it. Many pad their schedules, so a slow flight can still land "on time." But maybe you're thinking: that's governments and giant companies. I'm just late for brunch. So let's shrink it down. To one student, and one deadline.

In 1994, Canadian researchers asked thirty-seven psychology students about their final-year thesis. When will you finish? Give us your best guess. On average, they said about thirty-four days. Then the researchers asked a sneakier question. What if everything goes as badly as it possibly could? Their worst-case guess was about forty-nine days. So, how long did it really take? About fifty-five days. Later than their own worst-case scenario. Think about that. They planned for disaster, and reality was worse than the disaster. Only about thirty percent finished by the day they'd predicted. And many of them knew they were usually late. They just didn't think it would happen this time. Researchers call that the inside view. You picture this task, this week, going smoothly. You don't picture the printer jam, the friend who calls, or the bus that never comes. So why don't we learn from the last hundred times? Because your brain has been quietly lying to you about all of them.

Lie number one: your memory. In 2005, psychologists reviewed the research on how we predict time. We base our guesses on how long things took before. But we remember those past tasks as shorter than they really were. Your morning routine might really take forty minutes. Your memory might say twenty-five. So you plan tomorrow using the wrong number. Every single day. Two years later, the same researchers tested it. The people who remembered past tasks as shortest underestimated the next one the most.

Lie number two: your sense of a minute. Researchers at San Diego State asked people to sit quietly and guess when one minute had passed. Hurried "Type A" people called it at about fifty-eight seconds. Relaxed "Type B" people waited until about seventy-seven seconds. That's almost twenty seconds of difference, in a single minute. When one person thinks the minute is up, the other still has twenty seconds to go. Try it yourself. Close your eyes, count to what feels like one minute, then check a clock. Most people are off. And now you know which way you lean. So "five minutes" literally doesn't mean the same thing to everyone.

Lie number three: "I'll just do one more thing." One more email. One more episode. One more look for the keys. Each one feels like it takes no time at all. Stack three of them, and you're fifteen minutes late before you've found your shoes.

And then there's culture. Psychologist Robert Levine measured the pace of life in thirty-one countries. He timed people walking, timed post office clerks, and checked the public clocks. Switzerland came out fastest, with Japan and Western Europe close behind. In some places, five minutes late is nothing. In others, it's an insult. So part of your clock was set before you could even read one.

Now go back to that friend at the restaurant. They don't see your memory bias, your minute, or your culture. They see someone who said five minutes. Ten minutes late, three times a week, adds up to about twenty-six hours a year. A whole day of someone else's life. And it feels even longer for them. In 1985, researcher David Maister wrote a famous paper on the psychology of waiting. His rule: empty time feels longer than busy time. Your ten minutes fly by. Theirs crawl. It sounds hopeless. But it wasn't over yet.

So is a late person doomed to be late forever? No. But here's what doesn't work: trying harder. Kahneman's team heard the warning and still took eight years. What works is changing how you plan. Fix one: break it down. In 2004, a study found that listing every step made people's time estimates longer and less optimistic. The paper's title says it all: "If you don't want to be late, enumerate." Shower. Get dressed. Find keys. Walk to the car. Find parking. Every step you write down is one your brain had skipped. Fix two: plan backwards. A 2016 study found that working back from the deadline made predictions less optimistic. Start with "I need to be in my seat at nine," and walk it back. And fix three is the question I promised you. The one Kahneman's expert answered. Don't ask, "How long will this take?" Ask, "How long did this take last time?" Then time your morning once, with a stopwatch, from alarm to front door. Most people are shocked by the number. Plan with that one, not the hopeful one. And add one "one more thing" on top, because you know it's coming. Bonus trick: put the time you need to leave in your calendar, not the time you need to arrive.

So here's the twist. Many chronically late people aren't careless at all. They're optimists. They really believe the best version of the day is the one that's coming. Honestly, as mistakes go, that's a pretty nice one to make. In 1999, Sydney asked Utzon to come back and help shape the building's future. In 2007, his opera house became a World Heritage site. He died the following year. Today, nobody remembers it was ten years late. But your friend at the restaurant will remember the twenty minutes. Because your five minutes and their five minutes were never the same five minutes.

In 1957, Sydney picked a design for a new opera house. The plan said it would be finished in 1963, for about seven million dollars. It opened in 1973. Ten years late. And it cost about one hundred and two million dollars. A whole city was late, for a decade. By the end of this video, you'll know why your brain does the same thing every single morning.

And the architect paid for it. Jørn Utzon quit the project in 1966, after a fight over money with the state government. He went home to Denmark. He never saw the finished building in person. When the Queen opened it in 1973, he reportedly wasn't even watching on TV.

In 1979, two psychologists gave this mistake a name. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky called it the planning fallacy. We plan for the best-case version of the future, and then we're surprised by the real one. That same year, the writer Douglas Hofstadter published a joke that became famous. "It always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account Hofstadter's Law." It's a joke. It's also the most accurate thing in this video. And here's the irony. Years earlier, Kahneman had fallen for it himself. He was on a team writing a new school textbook in Israel. After a year of work, he asked everyone how long they thought it would take to finish. The answers clustered around two years. Then he asked the team's curriculum expert a different question. How long did other teams like ours take? The expert went quiet. Then he admitted that about forty percent of those teams never finished at all. And the ones that did took seven to ten years. So how did their own team compare? "Below average," the expert said. "But not by much." Kahneman's team took eight years. The man who would later win a Nobel Prize for studying bad judgment couldn't predict his own project.

And it isn't one unlucky team. The economist Bent Flyvbjerg collected data on more than sixteen thousand big projects, in over a hundred countries. Only about eight and a half percent finished on time and on budget. Just half a percent also delivered everything they promised. He calls it the iron law: over budget, over time, over and over again. Boston's "Big Dig," a highway tunnel project, was first budgeted at about two point eight billion dollars. It finished years late, in 2007, at about fourteen point six billion.

So is it just big projects? Or does it happen to everyone? In 1994, Canadian researchers asked thirty-seven psychology students about their final-year thesis. When do you think you'll finish? Give us your best guess. On average, they said about thirty-four days. Then the researchers asked a sneakier question. What if everything goes as badly as it possibly could? Their worst-case guess was about forty-nine days. The real answer? About fifty-five days. They finished later than their own worst-case scenario. Only about thirty percent finished by the day they had predicted. And here's the human detail. These students knew they were usually late. When asked, they admitted most of their past work ran over. They just didn't think it would happen this time. Researchers call that the inside view. When you plan, you picture this task, this week, going smoothly. You don't picture the printer jam, the friend who calls, or the bus that never comes. So why can't we learn from the last hundred times?

One reason is that your memory is lying to you. In 2005, a team of psychologists reviewed the research on how we predict time. Their argument was simple. We base our guesses on how long things took before. But we remember those past tasks as shorter than they really were. So if your morning routine really takes forty minutes, your memory might say twenty-five. And you plan the next morning using the wrong number. Every single day. Two years later, the same researchers tested it directly. The people who remembered past tasks as shortest were the ones who underestimated the next task the most.

A second reason is how you feel a minute. Researchers at San Diego State asked people to sit quietly and guess when one minute had passed. Hurried, competitive "Type A" people called time at about fifty-eight seconds. More relaxed "Type B" people waited until about seventy-seven seconds. For some people, a minute simply feels longer. So "I'll be there in five minutes" really means something different to each of them. When one person thinks a minute is up, the other still has almost twenty seconds to go.

And then there's the trap everyone knows. "I'll just do one more thing before I leave." One more email. One more episode. One more look for the keys. Each one feels like it takes no time at all, because in your head, it doesn't. But each "quick" thing really takes a few minutes. Stack three of them, and you're fifteen minutes late before you've even found your shoes.

Where you live matters too. In the 1990s, psychologist Robert Levine measured the pace of life in thirty-one countries. His team timed people walking in city centres, timed post office clerks, and checked the public clocks. Switzerland came out fastest, with Japan and Western Europe close behind. In some countries, a public clock that's five minutes off barely matters. In others, being five minutes late is an insult. So "late" isn't only about you. It's also about the clock your culture runs on.

But here's the uncomfortable part. The person waiting for you doesn't see your memory bias or your culture. They see someone who didn't show up when they said. And over time, that can quietly cost trust. Do the maths. Ten minutes late, three times a week, adds up to about twenty-six hours a year. That's a whole day of other people's lives, spent waiting. But it wasn't over yet.

So are late people doomed? Not at all. But trying harder doesn't help. Kahneman's team heard the expert's warning, kept going, and still took eight years. What helps is changing how you plan. The first fix is to break it down. In 2004, researchers found that when people listed every step of a task, their time estimates got longer. The title of their paper says it all: "If you don't want to be late, enumerate." Shower. Get dressed. Find keys. Walk to the car. Find parking. Each step you write down is a step your brain had quietly skipped. The second fix is to plan backwards. In 2016, a study found that planning from the deadline back to today led to less optimistic, more realistic predictions. Start with "I need to be in my seat at nine," and work back from there. The third fix is the one Kahneman's expert gave him. Don't ask "How long will this take me?" Ask "How long did this take last time?" And if you don't know, find out. Time your morning routine once, with a stopwatch, from alarm to front door. Most people are shocked by the number. That number, not the hopeful one, is the one to plan with. Then add one "one more thing" on top, because you know it's coming.

So here's the twist. Chronically late people are rarely the ones who don't care. Very often, they're optimists. They genuinely believe the best version of the day is the one that's going to happen. The Sydney Opera House is now one of the most famous buildings on Earth. Nobody remembers that it was ten years late. In 1999, the government asked Utzon to come back as a consultant on the building's future. In 2007, his opera house became a World Heritage site. He died the following year. But your friend at the restaurant will remember the twenty minutes.

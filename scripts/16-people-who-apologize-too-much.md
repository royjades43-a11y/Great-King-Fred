# Video 16: The Psychology of People Who Apologize Too Much

**Title:** The Psychology of People Who Apologize Too Much
**Length:** 1,444 words. That's about 8:35 at Josh's pace (video 15 ran 8:36 from 1,465 words), so it's over 8:00
and mid-roll ads are possible.

**First script under the storytelling rule (STYLE.md, Nov 2026):**
- No years and no researcher names in the voiceover. Every study is a scene the viewer walks through ("Picture a
  busy train station on a rainy day…").
- Names, years and journals live only in the description.
- On-screen study cards carry a story title ("The rain study"), not a citation.

## Why this title and topic

- **Search plus series:** "why do I say sorry so much" is searched constantly. The title keeps "The Psychology of
  People Who…" (47 characters, fits on phones).
- **Everyone does it:** saying sorry when someone else bumps into you, or apologizing to a chair. The viewer is caught
  in the first five seconds.
- **A story people retell:** on a rainy day, a stranger who said "I'm so sorry about the rain" got almost half of
  people to lend him their phone, against about 9 in 100 without it.
- **A practical payoff:** what makes an apology actually work (admit it, offer to fix it), when to say it (after
  listening), and when to swap "sorry" for "thank you".
- **Kind to the viewer:** over-apologizers are usually people who care. The aim is to make "sorry" mean something
  again, not to make anyone feel bad.

**Titles to test** (title playbook: search phrase first, then the finding):
- **Why You Say Sorry for Everything**: "why you…" search phrase, 32 characters. This is the strongest search
  candidate.
- **The Apology That Made Strangers Hand Over Their Phones**: leads with the finding, for browse and suggested.
- **How to Apologize the Right Way, According to Psychology**: a "how to" search phrase.

## Engagement design (`GROWTH.md`)

- **Opening:** the street bump, then "you said sorry to a chair". The promise at about 0:25: why we say sorry for
  everything, the apology that made strangers hand over their phones, and what makes an apology work.
- **Two open loops, both paid off:**
  - "A strange little apology that made strangers hand over their phones" → paid at about 1:20
  - "What makes an apology actually work" → paid at about 4:30
- **A re-hook around minute 4:** "So if apologies are this powerful, why do so many of them still fall flat?"
- **"But it wasn't over yet."** at about 5:35, before the last third.
- **A question roughly every 90 seconds**, plus one sincere question to the viewer: "What's the strangest thing
  you've ever apologized to?"
- **Irony and human detail:**
  - you say sorry when someone else steps on your foot
  - apologizing to a chair
  - "the rain had nothing to do with him"
  - refusing to apologize feels good, for a while
  - "I'm sorry you feel that way"
  - the forgotten birthday
  - "permission to say nothing at all" to the chair
- **Running image:** the chair opens the video and closes it.

chapter happens somewhere.

## Sources (for the description only, never read out)

| Story in the script | Source |
| --- | --- |
| The sorry diary: women reported more apologies, but men apologized just as often for what they saw as offenses; men rated imagined offenses as less severe | Schumann & Ross (2010), *Psychological Science* 21(11), 1649–1655, "Why women apologize more than men: Gender differences in thresholds for perceiving offensive behavior" |
| The rain study: "I'm so sorry about the rain" before asking to borrow a phone; about 47% lent it, against 9% without; superfluous apologies increased trust in other studies | Brooks, Dai & Schweitzer (2014), *Social and Personality Psychology Science* 5(4), 467–474, "I'm sorry about the rain! Superfluous apologies demonstrate empathic concern and increase trust" |
| The careless mistake: an apology after a harm reduced anger and aggression and improved impressions | Ohbuchi, Kameda & Agarie (1989), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 56(2), 219–227, "Apology as aggression control: Its role in mediating appraisal of and response to harm" |
| The refusal study: refusing to apologize increased feelings of power and, for a time, self-worth | Okimoto, Wenzel & Hedrick (2013), *European Journal of Social Psychology* 43(1), 22–31, "Refusing to apologize can have psychological benefits (and we issue no mea culpa for this research finding)" |
| The six-part apology: acknowledging responsibility mattered most, then an offer of repair; asking for forgiveness mattered least | Lewicki, Polin & Lount (2016), *Negotiation and Conflict Management Research* 9(2), 177–196, "An exploration of the structure of effective apologies" |
| Timing: later apologies were more effective because the victim felt heard | Frantz & Bennigson (2005), *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 41(2), 201–207, "Better late than early: The influence of timing on apology effectiveness" |
| Sorry to yourself: self-compassion after a transgression increased motivation to make amends and not repeat it | Breines & Chen (2012), *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* 38(9), 1133–1143, "Self-compassion increases self-improvement motivation" |

**Careful wording kept on purpose:**
- "Almost half" for 47%, and "about nine out of every hundred" for 9%.
- "The researchers think that little apology showed he cared": the authors' explanation is empathic concern.
- "Swap sorry for thank you" is offered as a suggestion to try, not as a research finding.
- "A pair of researchers in Canada": the diary study was run in Canada. No names.

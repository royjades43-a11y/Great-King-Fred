# Video 10: The Psychology of People Who Keep Going Back to Their Ex

**Title:** The Psychology of People Who Keep Going Back to Their Ex
**Length:** about 9:05 at the channel's pace (1,481 words); over 8:00 so mid-roll ads are possible

**How it differs from competitors:** most videos on this topic are either "how to get your ex back" or "never
go back, they're toxic". This one shows what researchers measured: brain scans of heartbreak, how memory
edits a relationship, what checking an ex's profile does, and what happens to couples who break up and make
up. It lands kindly. Going back isn't weakness, and it isn't always a mistake. But missing someone isn't a
reason on its own. Ask what's different this time.

**Engagement design:**
- **Talks straight to the viewer throughout:** "but for you", "if that's you", "be honest", "your ex already
  knows your coffee order".
- **Opens on the viewer's own late-night moment:** "Hey, stranger."
- **An open loop paid off later:** "the one question that tells a second chance from a second mistake" is answered at 6:50, then
  again in rule one and the ending.
- **A running image:** the highlight reel. It opens in the memory section, returns with the profile-checking
  and "that's the highlight reel talking", and closes the video ("a person, not a highlight reel").
- **A counter-intuitive turn:** rejection makes you want them more.
- **Every section ends on a hook.**

Lines in *[brackets]* are visual notes for the edit; they are not read out. Numbers and years are written
the way they are spoken, so `scripts/10-back-to-your-ex-voiceover.txt` can be pasted straight into
ElevenLabs.

**Visual style:** same as video 9. No chapter cards. One text zone at the top with short phrases, never full
captions. The stage below is for stick figures and drawings, with gentle motion timed to the words.

## [0:00–1:00] HOOK

It's late.
You're lying in bed, scrolling.
*[a stick figure in bed, lit by a phone; a small moon in the window]*
And there it is.
Their name.
*[a notification pops up on the phone: just a name, with a red dot]*
You open the chat.
You check when they were last online.
Three minutes ago.
You type, "Hey, stranger."
*[the message types out letter by letter in a chat bubble]*
You know how this ended last time.
You remember the fights, the silence, the night one of you finally said, "I'm done."
*[three quick flashes behind the figure: a storm cloud, an empty chair, a slammed door]*
But for you, it doesn't feel like going back.
It feels like going home.
*[top: "but for **you**, it feels like going **home**"; a little house glows on the phone screen]*
If that's you, you're far from alone.
Breaking up and making up is much more common than most people think.
And for some of us, it happens again and again.
*[a loop arrow circles two stick figures: apart, together, apart, together]*
Sound familiar?
Psychologists have scanned the brains of people who just got dumped.
They've followed couples who break up and make up.
And they've studied what happens when you keep checking your ex's profile.
*[three small icons line up as they're named: a brain, a loop, a phone with an eye]*
By the end of this video, you'll know why your ex is so hard to quit.
And the one question that tells a second chance from a second mistake.
*[top: "**one** question"; a large red question mark]*
Let's start with what a breakup does to your brain.

## [1:00–2:35] YOUR BRAIN ON HEARTBREAK

In twenty eleven, psychologist Ethan Kross and his team recruited forty people.
All of them had been through an unwanted breakup in the last six months.
*[label: STUDY · Kross et al., 2011 · 40 people]*
Inside a brain scanner, they looked at a photo of their ex and thought about the breakup.
*[a stick figure inside a scanner ring; a photo frame with a cracked heart]*
In a separate task, the researchers pressed a hot probe against their forearm.
Painful, but bearable.
*[a probe touches the arm; little red heat lines; the figure winces]*
Then they compared the two.
Some of the same areas lit up for both.
Areas linked to the physical feeling of pain.
*[two brains side by side, "ex" and "heat"; the same small region glows red in both]*
So when you say a breakup hurts, it may be more than a figure of speech.
A year earlier, anthropologist Helen Fisher and her team scanned fifteen people who'd just been dumped.
*[label: STUDY · Fisher et al., 2010 · 15 people]*
They were still intensely in love.
On average, the breakup was about two months old.
When they saw their ex's photo, the brain's reward system lit up.
Including areas linked to craving and addiction.
*[the brain glows; a craving meter beside it rises]*
The researchers described romantic rejection as a kind of addiction.
And here's the irony.
Being rejected didn't make them want their ex less.
In many ways, it made them want them more.
*[top: "rejected → wanted **more**"; the ex walks away and the heart pulses bigger]*
Fisher called it frustration attraction.
The person who caused the pain becomes the one thing your brain wants to stop it.
So is it any wonder you reach for your phone at one in the morning?
*[back in bed: a clock shows 1:00 and a hand reaches for the phone]*
But there was good news hidden in that study too.
The longer it had been since the breakup, the weaker the activity in an area linked to attachment.
*[the craving meter slowly falls as calendar pages flip]*
The craving fades.
It just doesn't fade on your schedule.

## [2:35–4:10] THE HIGHLIGHT REEL

So the pain is real.
But why does going back feel like such a good idea?
Part of the answer is your memory.
Psychologists have found that the feelings attached to bad memories usually fade faster than the feelings attached to good ones.
*[two memory cards: a red "good" card stays bright while a grey "bad" card fades]*
It's called the fading affect bias.
Most of the time, that's a gift.
It's how we move on from bad days.
But after a breakup, it plays a trick on you.
Your mind starts cutting a highlight reel.
*[a film strip rolls in; top: "the **highlight** reel"]*
The road trip.
The inside jokes.
The way they said your name.
*[frames pop into the strip: a car, two laughing sticks, a heart]*
Not the fight in the car on that same road trip.
*[one frame shows the same car with a storm cloud; scissors snip it out]*
You might even miss the things you used to complain about.
The snoring.
The terrible playlist.
*[a sleeping stick with "zzz"; a music note with a red cross that turns into a heart]*
You remember why you loved them.
You forget why you left.
Then loneliness adds its own voice.
Psychologist Stephanie Spielmann and her colleagues studied people's longing for their exes.
*[label: STUDY · Spielmann et al., 2016]*
People with a stronger fear of being single longed for their ex more.
They were also more likely to try to get back together.
It didn't matter who ended it.
*[a lone stick figure on a bench, with an empty seat next to them]*
And there's one more pull.
Communication researcher René Dailey has studied on-again, off-again couples for years.
One of the most common reasons people give for going back is simple.
Lingering feelings.
Another is familiarity.
Starting over with someone new is hard work.
Your ex already knows your coffee order.
*[a coffee cup slides across the table to the stick figure; a small red heart rises from the steam]*
So here's a question worth asking yourself.
Do you miss them, or do you miss being known?
*[top: "them, or being **known**?"]*

## [4:10–4:50] THE CHECKING

Now, maybe you haven't texted them.
But be honest.
Do you check their profile?
*[the stick figure's thumb scrolls a phone; an eye icon blinks]*
In twenty twelve, psychologist Tara Marshall surveyed four hundred and sixty-four people about their exes on Facebook.
*[label: STUDY · Marshall, 2012 · 464 people]*
The more people checked their ex's page, the worse they were doing.
More distress about the breakup.
More longing for their ex.
And less personal growth.
*[three small bars: distress up, longing up, growth down]*
Seeing an ex in person had a similar link.
More contact, more longing.
Now, these were correlations.
People who are struggling may simply check more.
But it's hard to heal a wound you reopen every night.
*[a bandage on a heart peels back each time the phone lights up]*
Every scroll through their photos adds another clip to the highlight reel.

## [4:50–6:00] WHAT HAPPENS WHEN PEOPLE GO BACK

So what happens when people do go back?
In twenty thirteen, a team led by sociologist Sarah Halpern-Meekin studied almost eight hundred young adults.
*[label: STUDY · Halpern-Meekin et al., 2013 · 792 young adults]*
More than four in ten had broken up and got back together at least once.
*[ten stick figures; just over four of them turn red, each with a little loop arrow]*
The researchers called it relationship churning.
Think about your own friends.
You can probably name a couple like that.
And it doesn't stop at dating.
In twenty fourteen, Amber Vennum and her colleagues studied more than a thousand couples who lived together or were married.
*[label: STUDY · Vennum et al., 2014 · 1,000+ couples]*
Over a third of the couples living together had broken up and made up.
So had about one in five married couples.
*[two bars: "living together · 1 in 3" and "married · 1 in 5"]*
Couples who'd cycled before the wedding started their marriages less satisfied.
They argued more.
And they were more likely to have a trial separation in the first three years.
*[two rings; a small crack appears between them]*
Couples who'd cycled once were also more likely to cycle again.
In twenty eighteen, a study led by Kale Monk linked more on-off cycling to more psychological distress.
*[the loop arrow spins faster; a tired stick figure slumps]*
So the pattern can wear you down.
Even when each reunion feels like coming home.
But it wasn't over yet.

## [6:00–7:20] WHEN GOING BACK WORKS

Because not every couple who goes back ends up where they started.
On average, on-again, off-again couples report lower satisfaction and more uncertainty.
But some of them do well.
The difference often comes down to one thing.
Whether anything actually changed.
*[top: "did anything **change**?"]*
Some couples split over distance, bad timing or a job in another city.
If that problem is gone, going back can make sense.
*[a plane and a long road shrink away; two figures step closer]*
Researchers who study couples talk about sliding versus deciding.
Sliding is drifting back because it's late, you're lonely, and they texted first.
*[a stick figure slides down a slope towards a phone]*
Deciding is choosing it with your eyes open.
So be honest.
Are you deciding right now, or sliding?
*[the figure stands at a fork with a checklist, eyes wide open]*
Vennum's team found that cycling couples tended to rush into big steps, like moving in together.
*[a house with a moving box that arrives far too fast]*
So here's the one question I promised you.
What's different this time?
*[top, large: "what's **different** this time?"]*
Not how you feel.
What's different.
If the honest answer is "I missed them," that's the highlight reel talking.
*[the film strip plays in a thought cloud]*
If you can both name what changed, and you can see it, that's a second chance.
Real change looks like a hard conversation about why it ended.
New habits, not new promises.
Time apart that you both actually used.
*[three ticks appear: a talk bubble, a calendar with habits, an hourglass]*
So how do you know which one you're in?

## [7:20–8:25] FIVE RULES

Rule one: ask the question.
Before you reply, write down what's different this time.
If you can't fill one line, don't send the text.
*[a note with one empty line; the send button greys out]*

Rule two: watch the whole movie.
Write down why it ended.
The real reasons, not the polished version.
Read it when the highlight reel starts playing.
*[the film strip unrolls to full length, storm clouds included]*

Rule three: mute, don't monitor.
You don't have to block them.
But stop checking.
Mute them, so their life isn't the first thing you see each morning.
Every look reopens the wound.
*[a speaker icon with a red slash over the phone; a sun rises on a calm screen]*

Rule four: ride out the craving.
Remember the brain scans.
The pull gets weaker with time.
Cravings come in waves.
Most waves pass.
When the urge hits, wait twenty minutes.
Call a friend instead.
*[a timer counts down; the stick figure calls a friend and both smile]*

And rule five: fill the space they left.
Your friends, your plans, the parts of you that went quiet.
*[the empty seat on the bench fills with a friend, a guitar, a plant]*
In one study, psychologists Ty Tashiro and Patricia Frazier found that growth after a breakup was common.
Many people said they'd become more confident and more independent.
*[label: STUDY · Tashiro & Frazier, 2003]*

## [8:25–9:10] THE TWIST AND THE ENDING

So here's the twist.
Missing your ex doesn't mean you should go back.
It means you loved someone.
And your brain is doing exactly what brains do after a loss.
People who keep going back usually aren't weak.
Very often, they're loyal.
They hold on, because holding on is what they do best.
Loyalty is a good thing.
Pointed at the wrong person, it keeps you stuck.
*[two hands holding a rope; the rope turns into a film strip]*
Just make sure you're holding on to a person, not a highlight reel.
The next time their name lights up your phone, remember the whole movie.
Then ask what's different this time.
If nothing is, you already have your answer.
*[the phone lights up with the name again; the stick figure puts it face down and walks to the window as the sun comes up]*

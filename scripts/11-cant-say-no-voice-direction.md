# Voice direction: "The Psychology of People Who Can't Say No"

A line-by-line reading guide, in the same format as videos 5 to 10 (see `scripts/05-always-late-voice-direction.md`
for the 12 rules and the full legend). It works for a human narrator, ElevenLabs or AI Studio (use each
section's **Direction** line as the style prompt), and for our free AI voice through `voice/directed.py`.

| Mark | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **word** | stress it |
| `\|` / `\|\|` | short / long beat |
| ↘ / ↗ | land down / lift (a genuine question) |
| (brisk) (slower) (land it) (lean in) (warm) (smile) (light) (character) | how to deliver the line |

Numbers are written the way they are spoken.

## Energy map

```
energy  5 |
        4 |  *              *  *                       *  *
        3 |     *  *     *        *     *  *     *  *        *
        2 |  *        *              *        *                 *  *
        1 |
          +----------------------------------------------------------
            hook   belong   autopilot   future you   who's asked   cost   rules  twist end
```

The hook is warm and a little rueful: a tired person on a couch, typing "Sure!" against their own wishes. The
belonging section is thoughtful, then relieved when the fear turns out to be overblown. The autopilot
section is the fun one: play the copy-machine reason and the word "pickle" with a smile. "Future you" is
knowing and dry. "Who gets asked" is fair and calm, never preachy. The cost section is honest but kind, and the
"I don't" study is bright. The rules are practical, and the twist is soft and generous.

This script talks straight to the viewer ("but for you", "think about your own workplace", "would you stop
being their friend?"). Read those asides as if to one friend, a little closer than the lines around them.

---

## 1 · Hook (0:00–0:55)
**Direction:** warm and rueful, like telling a friend about their own Thursday night. Read the text message
in a bright "friend" voice, and "Sure! What time?" a touch too cheerful. "But for you" is personal and close.
A real smile on "Sound familiar?". Energy 2, rising to 4 by the promise.

(slower) It's Thursday **night**. | You're finally on the **couch**. | Your phone **buzzes**. ||
(character) "Hey! | Any chance you could help me **move** on Saturday?" ||
(warm) You were going to sleep **in**. | You were going to do nothing at **all**. |
(character) You type, | "**Sure**! | What time?" ||
(lean in) And the second you hit **send**, | a tiny voice says, | "Why did I just **do** that?" ||
(smile) Sound **familiar**? ↗ ||
You're not **lazy**, | and you're not **selfish**. |
(lean in) But for **you**, | no doesn't feel like a small **word**. | It feels like a fight you're **starting**. ||
(warm) If that's you, | you're in good **company**. |
(brisk) Psychologists have studied why no is so hard to **say**. |
They've asked strangers to write in a library **book**. |
They've tested which refusals actually **work**. |
And they've found out who gets asked the **most**. ||
By the end of this video, | you'll know why yes slips out | before you can **stop** it. |
(lean in) And the **one** small word swap | that makes no much **easier**. ||
Let's start with why no feels so **dangerous**. ||

## 2 · Why no feels dangerous (0:55–2:25)
**Direction:** thoughtful at first, a storyteller. Lift into relief on "the fear is usually much bigger than
the fallout". The irony ("we just forget it") gets a knowing smile. Energy 3.

For most of human history, | being pushed out of the group | was **dangerous**. |
Alone, | you were far less likely to **survive**. ||
(brisk) In nineteen ninety-five, | psychologists Roy **Baumeister** and Mark **Leary** | called this the need to **belong**. |
They argued it's one of our most **basic** human drives. ||
(lean in) So when someone asks you for something, | part of your brain hears a **different** question. |
(slower) Do you still want to be part of the **group**? ↗ ||
No can feel like the **wrong** answer. |
(light) Even when the request is just a **Saturday**. ||

(lean in) But here's the **thing**. |
The fear is usually much **bigger** | than the **fallout**. ||
(brisk) In twenty twenty-three, | marketing professors Julian **Givi** and Colleen **Kirk** | ran five experiments | with more than two **thousand** people. |
Some imagined being **invited** to something. |
(light) A museum **exhibit**. | A dinner with a celebrity **chef**. |
Others imagined being the one who sent the **invitation**. ||
The people saying no | expected the **worst**. |
They thought their friend would be **angry**, | **hurt**, | and less likely to invite them **again**. |
The people who got the no? ↗ | (light) Mostly, | they were **fine**. ||
(smile) Here's the **irony**. |
We all know how little a no bothers us | when we're the one **asking**. |
We just **forget** it | the moment we're the one **saying** it. |
In one experiment, | people first got turned down **themselves**. |
After that, | they worried **less** about saying no to others. ||
(lean in) So why does yes keep slipping out **anyway**? ↗ ||

## 3 · Yes on autopilot (2:25–4:05)
**Direction:** the fun section. Brisk and curious on the setup, then play the copy-machine reason dead-pan
and let a smile in on "Of course they had to make copies." Say "pickle" plainly, as if it were serious science.
The closing question is quiet and direct. Energy 4.

Part of it is **speed**. |
Most yeses aren't **decisions**. | (land it) They're **reflexes**. ||
(brisk) In the late nineteen seventies, | psychologist Ellen **Langer** and her colleagues | ran a sneaky experiment | at a university copy **machine**. |
A stranger walked up to someone about to make copies | and asked to go **first**. |
(character) "Excuse me, | I have five pages. | May I use the copy **machine**?" |
About **sixty** percent let them. ||
Then the stranger added a **reason**. |
(character) "Because I'm in a **rush**." |
About ninety-**four** percent said yes. ||
(smile) Now the **funny** part. |
Another version used a reason | that wasn't a reason at **all**. |
(character) "May I use the copy machine, | because I have to make **copies**?" ||
(smile) Of **course** they had to make copies. | Everyone at the machine had to make copies. |
It still got a yes | about ninety-**three** percent of the time. ||
(lean in) The word **because** | did most of the work. |
Your brain heard the **shape** of a reason, | and it said yes on **autopilot**. ||

And that autopilot goes **further** than you'd think. |
(brisk) In twenty fourteen, | psychologist Vanessa **Bohns** and her colleagues | sent students out with an odd **request**. |
They asked strangers to write one word in pen | in a library **book**. |
(light) The word was **pickle**. ||
Before they started, | the students guessed | that about twenty-**eight** percent would agree. |
(lean in) In fact, | more than sixty-**four** percent | wrote pickle in the **book**. ||
Many of them **hesitated**. | Some even called it **vandalism**. |
(slower) And then they did it **anyway**. ||
Because saying no to someone's **face** is awkward. |
(land it) And for a lot of us, | awkward feels worse than **wrong**. ||
(lean in) So think about the last time you said **yes**. |
Did **you** decide, ↗ | or did your **mouth** decide for you? ↗ ||

## 4 · The future-you trap (4:05–5:05)
**Direction:** knowing and dry. A little smile on "future you is just you". Energy 3.

There's another **trap**, | and it's hiding in your **calendar**. ||
(brisk) In two thousand five, | marketing researchers Gal **Zauberman** and John **Lynch** | studied how people think about future **time**. |
They asked people how much spare time they'd have **today**. |
And how much they'd have a month from **now**. |
Most people expected to have more free time | in the **future**. |
They were more optimistic about future **time** | than about future **money**. ||
(lean in) But next month always arrives | just as **busy** as this one. ||
So a favor in five weeks | feels **free**. |
(light) It's a problem for **future** you. |
(smile) Then future you shows **up**, | and future you | is just **you**. |
Same **job**. | Same **tiredness**. | Same Saturday you wanted to **yourself**. ||
(light) It's been nicknamed | the "Yes, | **damn**" effect. |
You say **yes**. | Then the day comes, | and you say **damn**. ||
(lean in) How many things on your calendar right now | did past you agree **to**? ↗ ||

## 5 · Who gets asked (5:05–6:15)
**Direction:** fair and calm, never preachy. Give the numbers room. "Think about your own workplace" is close
and personal. Land "The reward for helping is more requests." Then a real pause and a lower, slower
"But it wasn't over yet." Energy 3.

(lean in) And here's something even less **fair**. |
The people who say yes the most | get asked the **most**. ||
(brisk) In twenty seventeen, | economist Linda **Babcock** and her colleagues | studied tasks nobody **wants**. |
The ones that need doing, | but never get you **promoted**. |
(light) Taking **notes**. | Planning the office **party**. | Sitting on one more **committee**. ||
In their experiments, | women volunteered for these tasks | more than **men**. |
And when they were asked **directly**, | women said yes | about seventy-**six** percent of the time. |
Men said yes | about fifty-**one** percent of the time. ||
But it wasn't just about who **volunteered**. |
Both men **and** women | were more likely to ask a **woman**. |
(land it) People asked the person | they expected to say **yes**. ||
(lean in) Think about your own **workplace**. | Your own **family**. | Your own group **chat**. |
Who always gets asked | to organize, | cover, | or fix it? ↗ ||
If it's **you**, | it may not be because you're the best at it. |
It may be because everyone knows | you won't say **no**. ||
(smile) That's the irony of being **reliable**. |
(land it) The reward for helping | is more **requests**. ||
(slower) But it wasn't **over** yet. ||

## 6 · What yes costs, and the word that helps (6:15–7:30)
**Direction:** honest but kind on the cost. Brighter for the "I don't" study, like sharing a good trick.
"People don't usually argue with who you are" lands with a small smile. Energy 3, lifting to 4.

Because all those yeses **add** up. |
(brisk) In two thousand five, | management researchers Mark **Bolino** and William **Turnley** | studied employees who regularly went above and **beyond**. |
(light) Staying **late**. | Taking on extra **work**. | Helping without being **asked**. |
Going the extra mile was linked | to more **overload** | and more job **stress**. |
And more conflict | between work and **home**. ||
(warm) Of course, | helping isn't the **problem**. |
Helping is one of the **best** things people do. |
(lean in) The problem is the yes | you didn't **choose**. |
Every yes to something you don't want | is a no to something you **do**. |
Your **sleep**. | Your **family**. | The project you keep pushing to next **month**. ||

(lean in) So how do you actually say **no**? ↗ ||
(brisk) In twenty twelve, | marketing professors Vanessa **Patrick** and Henrik **Hagtvedt** | tested two tiny **phrases**. |
(character) "I **can't**," | and "I **don't**." ||
In one study, | people practiced saying one of **them**. |
Then, on their way out, | they were offered a thank-you **snack**. |
(light) A chocolate **bar**, | or a granola **bar**. |
About sixty-**four** percent of the "I don't" group | picked the granola bar. |
Only about thirty-**nine** percent of the "I can't" group **did**. ||
"I can't" sounds like a **rule** someone gave you. |
So it invites a **negotiation**. | (character) "Can't you just this **once**?" ||
(lean in) "I don't" sounds like who you **are**. |
In a second paper, | the same researchers found that "I don't" refusals | persuaded **other** people too. |
They signaled **conviction**. |
(smile) People don't usually argue | with who you **are**. ||
So here's what you can **do** with all this. ||

## 7 · Five rules (7:30–8:35)
**Direction:** bright, practical, one rule at a time with a beat between them. Read the example sentences
in a friendly, natural voice, the way the viewer could say them. Energy 4.

(brisk) Rule **one**: | buy yourself **time**. |
Your first answer doesn't have to be yes or **no**. |
(character) Try, | "Let me check | and get back to **you**." |
That one sentence turns a **reflex** | into a **decision**. ||

Rule **two**: | make it **tomorrow**. |
Before you say yes to something next month, | ask one **question**. |
Would I say yes | if it were **tomorrow**? ↗ |
(land it) If the answer is **no**, | the answer is **no**. ||

Rule **three**: | say "I **don't**," | not "I **can't**." |
(character) "I don't take on new projects on **weekends**." | "I don't lend money to **friends**." |
It sounds like a **value**, | not an **excuse**. ||

Rule **four**: | keep it **short** and **kind**. |
You don't need a three-paragraph **excuse**. |
Long excuses invite people | to solve your problem **for** you. |
(character) "Thank you for **asking**. | I'm going to pass this **time**." |
(land it) That's **enough**. ||

(brisk) And rule **five**: | remember the other **side**. |
When you hesitate, | picture being the one who **asked**. |
(lean in) If a friend said no to you, | would you stop being their **friend**? ↗ |
(warm) Probably **not**. | They won't **either**. ||

## 8 · The twist and the ending (8:35–9:15)
**Direction:** the softest part. Slow and sure on the twist. "Very often, they're the kindest people in the
room" is warm and generous. The challenge and CTA are friendly and upbeat. Energy 2, then 4 for the
challenge.

(slower) So here's the **twist**. ||
People who can't say no | aren't **weak**. |
(warm) Very often, | they're the **kindest** people in the room. |
They notice what others **need**. | They hate letting anyone **down**. |
(warm) That's a beautiful **thing**. ||
(lean in) But a yes only means something | if a no was **possible**. |
If you say yes to **everything**, | your yes stops being a **gift**. | It becomes a **reflex**. |
(land it) Saying no sometimes | is what makes your yes worth **something**. ||
The next time your phone **buzzes**, | take a **breath**. |
Ask yourself if it were **tomorrow**. |
(slower) And if the honest answer is **no**, | say it **kindly**, | and let it **go**. ||

(brisk) So here's your **challenge** for this week. |
Say one small **no**. | Something **easy**. |
Then notice what **happens**. |
Come back and tell me if anyone even **minded**. ||

(smile) What's the hardest thing you've ever said **yes** to? ↗ | Tell me in the comments. |
Which habit should we cover **next**? ↗ | Drop it in the comments. ↘

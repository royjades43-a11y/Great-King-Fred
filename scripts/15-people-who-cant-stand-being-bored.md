# Video 15: The Psychology of People Who Can't Stand Being Bored

**Title:** The Psychology of People Who Can't Stand Being Bored
**Length:** 1,465 words. That's about 8:40 at Josh's pace (2.81 words a second on video 13; video 14 ran 8:37 from
1,471 words), so it's over 8:00 and mid-roll ads are possible.

## Why this title and topic

- **Search plus series:** "why can't I sit still / be bored" is searched constantly, and the title keeps the "The
  Psychology of People Who…" pattern (51 characters, fits on phones, no question mark).
- **Everyone does it, today:** reaching for your phone while the kettle boils. The viewer is caught in the first
  five seconds.
- **The most shareable finding we've used:** two out of three men chose to give themselves an electric shock rather
  than sit with their thoughts for 15 minutes, and one man pressed the button 190 times. People will send that to
  friends.
- **A kind, useful payoff:** boredom is a signal, not an enemy. A little of it makes you calmer and more creative,
  and people enjoy quiet thinking more than they expect to.
- **It follows video 14:** procrastination and phone-checking are close cousins, so the end screen can link the two.

**Alternate titles for Test & Compare:**
- Why You Can't Sit Alone With Your Thoughts
- People Chose Electric Shocks Over Their Own Thoughts
- Why Boredom Is Good for Your Brain

## Tone (locked from video 13)

- **Spoken, not written:** whole, natural sentences, the way you'd explain it to a friend across a table.
- **No "it isn't X, it's Y" formulas** and no strings of two-word fragments.
- **Every line speaks to the viewer** ("if that sounds like you", "have you ever…", "let me know").

## Engagement design (`GROWTH.md`)

- **Opening: the most relatable image first, then the promise.**
  - **0:00:** "You're waiting for the kettle to boil… before the first thirty seconds are up, your hand is already
    reaching for your phone."
  - **About 0:20:** "So in this video, we're going to look at why sitting alone with your thoughts feels so hard…" It
    states all three payoffs, spoken to the viewer.
  - **About 0:40:** the first real finding (Wilson 2014: the plain room).
- **Two open loops, both paid off:**
  - "Why so many people chose an electric shock over a few quiet minutes" → paid at about 1:15
  - "Why a little boredom might be one of the best things for your brain" → paid at about 6:00
- **A re-hook around minute 3:** "How many times a day do you reach for your phone without even deciding to?"
- **"But it wasn't over yet."** at about 5:45, before the last third.
- **A question roughly every 90 seconds**, plus one sincere question to the viewer: "What's the first thing you reach
  for when you're bored?"
- **Irony and human detail:**
  - two minutes of kettle, and you can't wait thirty seconds
  - a third of people cheated at home with nobody watching
  - people who'd pay to avoid a shock, then shocked themselves
  - "Yes, the same Timothy Wilson."
  - patting your pocket for a buzz that never came
  - we check our phones to feel less anxious, and it does the opposite
  - the best ideas arrive in the shower and over a sink full of dishes
- **Running image:** the kettle opens the video, returns as rule one ("Let the kettle boil") and closes it.

Numbers and years are written the way they are spoken, so `scripts/15-bored-voiceover.txt` can be pasted straight
into ElevenLabs. The reading guide is `scripts/15-bored-voice-direction.md`. Pronunciations: "Kushlev" is
KOOSH-lev, "Thuy-vy" is twee-vee, and "Murayama" is moo-rah-YAH-mah.

## Performance and art direction (STYLE.md, from video 13)

The host stick figure talks to the viewer, with a synced mouth and posture that acts out each line. Story figures act
out the studies, and every chapter happens somewhere.

| The good side | bathroom and outdoor walk | A phone book and two plastic cups with ideas popping out; a shower with a lightbulb; a calm figure sitting alone as storm lines smooth out; a "predicted / actual" enjoyment pair. |

Calm and warm, nothing dramatic: the shock is told with a raised eyebrow, with no big flashes or shaking.

## Sources

| Line | Source |
| --- | --- |
| Sitting alone with only your thoughts for 6–15 minutes was not very enjoyable and hard to concentrate; similar for community adults; about a third cheated in the at-home version | Wilson, Reinhard, Westgate, Gilbert, Ellerbeck, Hahn, Brown & Shaked (2014), *Science* 345(6192), 75–77, "Just think: The challenges of the disengaged mind" |
| After saying they'd pay to avoid the shock, 12 of 18 men (67%) and 6 of 24 women (25%) shocked themselves at least once in 15 minutes; one man 190 times | Same paper (Wilson et al., 2014), Study 10. The "new sensations" line is the authors' tentative explanation. |
| Boredom arises when attention isn't engaged (a mismatch with demands) or when the activity lacks meaning | Westgate & Wilson (2018), *Psychological Review* 125(5), 689–713, "Boring thoughts and bored minds: The MAC model of boredom and cognitive engagement" |
| 89% of undergraduates had experienced phantom vibrations; most weren't bothered | Drouin, Kaiser & Miller (2012), *Computers in Human Behavior* 28(4), 1490–1496 |
| A phone in another room vs face down on the desk: better working memory and fluid intelligence scores | Ward, Duke, Gneezy & Bos (2017), *Journal of the Association for Consumer Research* 2(2), 140–154, "Brain drain". Some later replications found weaker or no effects, so the script says so. |
| Checking email three times a day for a week lowered daily stress (crossover design) | Kushlev & Dunn (2015), *Computers in Human Behavior* 43, 220–228, "Checking email less frequently reduces stress" |
| Copying numbers from a phone book for 15 minutes led to more uses for a pair of polystyrene cups | Mann & Cadman (2014), *Creativity Research Journal* 26(2), 165–173, "Does being bored make us more creative?" |
| 15 minutes alone without devices reduced high-arousal feelings (positive and negative) and increased calm | Nguyen, Ryan & Deci (2018), *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* 44(1), 92–106, "Solitude as an approach to affective self-regulation" |
| People underestimated how much they'd enjoy 20 minutes of just thinking | Hatano, Ogulmus, Shigemasu & Murayama (2022), *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 151(12), 3172–3183, "Thinking about thinking: People underestimate how enjoyable and engaging just waiting is" |

**Careful wording kept on purpose:**
- "For most people, it wasn't very enjoyable" matches the paper's average ratings. It doesn't say people hated it.
- "Maybe because, for a lot of us, even a zap feels better than nothing at all" is framed as a maybe.
- The phone-on-the-desk result is followed by "to be fair, later studies haven't always found an effect this strong"
  and a careful version of the takeaway.
- "Thuy-vy Nguyen" has its full first name so the voice reads it as a name.

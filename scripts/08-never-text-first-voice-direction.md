# Voice direction: "The Psychology of People Who Never Text First"

A line-by-line reading guide, in the same format as videos 5 to 7 (see `scripts/05-always-late-voice-direction.md`
for the 12 rules and the full legend). It works for a human narrator, ElevenLabs or AI Studio (use each
section's **Direction** line as the style prompt), and for our free AI voice through `voice/directed.py`.

| Mark | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **word** | stress it |
| `\|` / `\|\|` | short / long beat |
| ↘ / ↗ | land down / lift (a genuine question) |
| (brisk) (slower) (land it) (lean in) (warm) (smile) (light) (character) | how to deliver the line |

Numbers are written the way they are spoken.

## Energy map

```
energy  5 |
        4 |  *     *                                  *  *
        3 |     *     *   *   *   *        *       *        *
        2 |                          *  *     *  *             *
        1 |
          +----------------------------------------------------
            hook  study 3 stories  cost  layers survey  rules twist end
```

The hook is knowing and a little playful (we're catching the viewer in the act). The three stories are
warm and reassuring. The cost section is the quietest and most serious. The rules are bright and practical,
and the twist lands warm and slow.

---

## 1 · Hook (0:00–0:40)
**Direction:** intimate and knowing, like describing something the viewer did last night. Light on the
excuses (each one a little more convincing to yourself). A real smile on "Sound familiar?". Energy 4.

(warm) You open a chat with an old **friend**. |
The last message is from four months **ago**. ||
(character) You type, | "Hey, how've you **been**?" | (light) Then you **delete** it. ||
(light) They're probably **busy**. | It's been too **long**. | It would be **weird**. |
(character) And anyway, | if they wanted to talk, | they'd text **you**. ||
(smile) Sound **familiar**? ↗ ||
(lean in) Here's the **strange** part. |
On the other side of that chat, | your friend may be doing the exact **same** thing. ||
Psychologists have now **tested** what happens | when people send that message. |
Across **thousands** of people, | the senders got it wrong in the **same** direction, | almost every time. ||
By the end of this video, you'll know **why**. |
(lean in) And why the friend you're waiting for | is probably waiting for **you**. ||

## 2 · The reach-out experiments (0:40–1:55)
**Direction:** a curious storyteller with good news to share. Clear and steady on the numbers; a small
lift on "Not once." The "Think about that." beat is the heart of the section. Energy 3, rising to 4.

(brisk) In twenty twenty-two, | a team led by psychologist Peggy **Liu** | ran a series of experiments on one simple act. ||
(land it) Reaching **out**. ||
More than **five thousand nine hundred** people took part, | across **thirteen** experiments. |
In the first, people remembered reaching out to someone, | or someone reaching out to **them**. |
People who remembered being contacted | rated their appreciation **higher** than the senders imagined. ||
In others, | people **really** did it. |
They sent a short note to someone they hadn't talked to in a **while**. |
(light) Sometimes with a small gift, | like **cookies**. ||
Then both sides answered the **same** question. |
How much did the person **appreciate** it? ||
(brisk) The senders **guessed**. | The receivers told the **truth**. ||
(land it) And the senders **underestimated** it. ||
(lean in) Not **once**. | Again and **again**, | across the studies. |
People liked being reached out to | far **more** than the person reaching out expected. ||
(lean in) Here's the part that matters **most**. |
The gap got **bigger** | when the message was more **surprising**. |
When it came from someone they weren't close to **anymore**. |
When it arrived out of the **blue**, | for no reason at **all**. ||
(slower) Think about **that**. ||
The thing that stops you from texting | is "it's been too **long**." |
(land it) And that is **exactly** what makes the message land. ||
(warm) The weirdness you're afraid of | is the best part of the **gift**. ||
(lean in) So if a text means that much, | why don't we **send** it? ↗ ||

## 3 · Three stories (1:55–3:55)
**Direction:** a kind, clear numbered list. Each "Story number…" is a fresh, slightly brighter start, and
the quoted story is said the way the viewer says it to themselves. The research lines are calm and sure;
the closing lines of each story land warm. Energy 3.

Because when you look at that empty chat, | your brain tells you **three** stories. |
(land it) And all three | are **wrong**. ||

(brisk) Story number **one**: | (character) "They don't want to hear from **me**." ||
(warm) If you've watched our video on small talk, | you'll know the liking **gap**. |
After a conversation, people consistently **underestimate** | how much the other person liked them. |
Even **dormmates** | who'd lived together for **months**. ||
We carry that same doubt into the **chat**. |
(light) We remember every awkward **pause**. | We forget the part where they **laughed**. |
And so we assume the silence means | they've moved **on**. ||
(warm) Very often, | it means they're **busy**. | Or they're waiting for **you**. |
(land it) A friend who hasn't texted in months | isn't a **verdict**. | It's a **gap**. ||

(brisk) Story number **two**: | (character) "It'll be **awkward**." ||
In twenty eighteen, psychologists Amit Kumar and Nicholas Epley | ran a simple **test**. |
People wrote a thank-you **letter** | to someone who'd helped them. |
Before sending it, | the writers predicted how the receiver would **feel**. |
They expected the letter to feel more **awkward** than it did. |
And they **underestimated** | how happy it would make the person. ||
(smile) Here's the **human** detail. |
The writers worried about getting the **words** right. |
The receivers barely **noticed** the words. | (warm) They noticed the **warmth**. ||
(land it) You're editing a **sentence**. | They're reading a **feeling**. ||
(smile) So when did you last delete a message | because it didn't sound **perfect**? ↗ ||

(brisk) Story number **three**: | (character) "If they cared, | they'd text **first**." ||
This one feels like **self-respect**. |
(character) You texted **last** time. | It's **their** turn. ||
Psychologists Margaret Clark and Judson Mills | described **two** kinds of relationships. |
In **exchange** relationships, | people keep **score**. |
(character) You did something for me, | so I **owe** you. |
(light) That's how **business** works. |
In **communal** relationships, | like friendships, | people give because they **care**, | not to settle a debt. ||
Their research suggests that keeping a strict score | is a sign of the **first** kind. |
(slower) So counting who texted last | quietly turns a friend into a **transaction**. ||
(lean in) And here's the **irony**. |
Your friend may be keeping the **same** score. |
Two people, | both waiting for the other to **prove** they care. |
(slower) Both feeling a little **forgotten**. ||
(land it) Neither one **forgot**. ||

## 4 · What it costs (3:55–5:55)
**Direction:** the quiet, honest middle. Start with a knowing smile on the "catch up" line, then slow down.
The hours and the layers are explained simply, like drawing on a napkin. The survey and health numbers are
said plainly, with no drama; let the numbers do the work. Energy 2–3.

(smile) Everyone knows this **line**. |
(character) "We should catch up **soon**!" | "**Definitely**!" ||
(light) And then nobody **does**. ||
Not because anyone stopped **caring**. |
Because both people assumed | the **other** one would plan it. ||
(slower) So what happens if nobody goes **first**? ↗ ||
It isn't **dramatic**. | (land it) That's the **problem**. ||
Friendships rarely end with a **fight**. |
(slower) They end with a pause | that gets **longer**. ||
In twenty eighteen, communication researcher Jeffrey **Hall** | estimated how much time friendship **takes**. |
About **fifty** hours | to go from acquaintance to casual friend. |
About **ninety** hours | to become a real friend. |
And more than **two hundred** hours | to become a **close** friend. ||
Those hours don't happen on their **own**. | Someone has to **start** them. ||
Psychologist Robin **Dunbar's** research | suggests our friendships sit in **layers**. |
About **five** people in the innermost circle. | About **fifteen** in the next. |
And those inner layers need regular **contact** to stay there. |
Roughly **weekly** for the closest five. | Roughly **monthly** for the next fifteen. ||
(slower) Friends don't usually **leave**. |
(land it) They slide **outward**, | one layer at a time. ||
Dunbar and his colleague Sam Roberts followed students | as they moved away to **university**, | for eighteen months. |
When contact with old friends **dropped**, | the friendships **faded**. |
Faster than **family** ties did. ||
And the friendships that survived | were the ones where someone made an **effort**. |
For the **women** in the study, | talking more on the phone helped. |
For the **men**, | doing things **together** did. ||
(slower) A lot of people are now feeling the **cost**. |
In a twenty twenty-one American survey, | **twelve** percent of adults said they had no close friends at all. |
In nineteen ninety, | it was **three** percent. |
Among men in that survey, | it was **fifteen** percent. ||
(lean in) And this isn't only about **feelings**. |
A review of one hundred and forty-eight studies, | covering more than **three hundred thousand** people, | found something striking. |
People with stronger social ties | had about **fifty percent** higher odds of survival | over the study periods. ||
In twenty twenty-three, America's Surgeon General | compared the health risk of disconnection | to smoking up to **fifteen** cigarettes a day. ||
(slower) All of that | can start with one message | nobody **sent**. ||
(lean in) But it wasn't over **yet**. ||

## 5 · How to text first (5:55–7:15)
**Direction:** relief, then a bright, practical coach. The opening callback is warm and simple. Each
"Rule…" is a clean new step with a smile; the example messages are said like real texts. Energy 4.

(warm) Remember what the receivers noticed | that the senders **missed**. |
Not the **gift**. | Not the **wording**. ||
(land it) The **surprise**. ||
The simple fact that someone **thought** of them, | when they didn't **have** to. |
That's what the friend you're waiting for wants **too**. |
Not a perfect **message**. | (warm) Proof that they crossed your **mind**. ||
(brisk) So how do you actually **do** it? ↗ ||
(brisk) Rule **one**: | don't wait for a **reason**. |
(character) "Saw this and thought of **you**" | is **enough**. |
(smile) Out of the blue is a **feature**, | not a bug. ||
(brisk) Rule **two**: | skip the **apology**. |
You don't need to explain the four **months**. |
(character) "It's been forever, | I miss talking to **you**" | works better than a paragraph about how busy you've been. ||
(brisk) Rule **three**: | say something **real**. |
(light) A meme is a **start**. |
But tell them one **true** thing, | or ask one **real** question. |
Instead of (character) "how are **you**?", | try (character) "what's been the **best** part of your month?" |
Research on conversations suggests | people want more **depth** than we expect. ||
(brisk) Rule **four**: | stop keeping **score**. |
If you're the one who usually texts first, | that doesn't make you **needy**. |
(warm) It makes you the friend | who keeps the friendship **alive**. ||
(brisk) Rule **five**: | make it a **habit**, | not a mood. |
Pick **one** person a week. | **One** message. |
It takes about **ten seconds**. |
Those ten seconds add up | to the **hours** that friendship needs. ||
(smile) And the best part? ↗ |
The research suggests the message doesn't have to be **perfect**. |
(land it) It just has to be **sent**. ||

## 6 · The twist and the ending (7:15–8:05)
**Direction:** the softest part. Slow and sure on the twist; "Why not you?" is a gentle, real question,
not a challenge. The challenge and CTA are friendly and upbeat. Energy 2, then 4 for the challenge.

(slower) So here's the **twist**. ||
People who never text first | usually aren't **cold**. |
(warm) Very often, | they care a **lot**. |
They're just afraid of being the one | who cares **more**. ||
But the research says the person on the other side of that chat | is probably not **annoyed**. |
They're probably not busy **forever**. |
(warm) And they'd probably love to hear from **you**. ||
(slower) They might be staring at the same empty chat, | waiting for **you**. ||
Someone has to go **first**. |
(land it) Why not **you**? ↗ ||

(brisk) So here's your **challenge** for today. |
Think of **one** person you've lost touch with. |
Send them **one** message. | No reason **needed**. |
Then come back and tell me what they **said**. ||

(smile) Are you the friend who always texts **first**, | or the one who **waits**? ↗ | Tell me in the comments. |
Which habit should we cover **next**? ↗ | Drop it in the comments. ↘

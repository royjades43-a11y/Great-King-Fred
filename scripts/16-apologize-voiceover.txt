Someone bumps into you in the street. And somehow, you're the one who says sorry. You say sorry to the waiter when your food is wrong. You say sorry when someone else steps on your foot. You say sorry before you ask a question. And once, maybe, you said sorry to a chair. Some of us say it so often, we don't even hear ourselves anymore. If that sounds like you, here's something nobody tells you. Every sorry you don't owe makes you a little smaller. So in this video, we're going to look at why some of us say sorry for almost everything. You'll find out about a strange little apology that made strangers hand over their phones. And by the end, you'll never apologize the same way again. Let's start with a simple question. Who says sorry the most? A pair of researchers in Canada asked people to keep a diary for twelve days. Every evening, they wrote down every time they had apologized that day. They also wrote down every time they thought they had done something wrong. Small things counted too. Bumping into someone. Running a few minutes late. Interrupting a friend. Women reported apologizing more often than men. But here's the part most people miss. Men apologized just as often as women, for the things they thought were wrong. They just thought fewer things needed an apology in the first place. Then they asked people to imagine small moments, like being a little rude to a friend by accident. The men tended to rate those moments as less serious. So the difference came down to where each person draws the line. So if you say sorry all the time, it's often because you notice more than other people do. That's a gift. You just need to stop spending it on chairs. Have you ever wondered where your own line sits? Here's where it gets interesting. Picture a busy train station on a rainy day. A young man walks up to strangers, one by one, and asks to borrow their phone. That's a big ask. Most people would say no, and most people did. Only about nine people out of every hundred handed it over. Then he changed one small thing. Before asking, he said, "I'm so sorry about the rain." He obviously didn't cause the rain. The rain had nothing to do with him. That's what makes it so strange. But suddenly, almost half of the strangers lent him their phone. Five words. Five times the trust. The researchers think that little apology showed he cared about how the other person was feeling. And people trust someone who seems to care. The same team found that people who made these small, extra apologies seemed warmer and easier to trust. So the word "sorry" can do a lot more than admit guilt. Very often, it's a small way of saying, "I see you." That's also why a real apology can calm someone down so quickly. In one experiment, a research assistant made a careless mistake that made people look bad on a task. Some people got an apology. Others got nothing. The people who heard "sorry" felt less angry. They liked the assistant more. And when they had a chance to get back at the assistant, they were much less likely to take it. So for most of us, saying sorry is a kind of social glue. Think about the last time someone cut in front of you in a queue. Then they turned and said, "Oh, sorry, I didn't see you." Most of the annoyance just melts away. It keeps small bumps from turning into big fights. But then there are people who almost never apologize. You probably know at least one. The person who would rather lose a friendship than say two small words. So a team of psychologists asked people to remember a time they had hurt someone. Some of them wrote an apology. Others wrote a message refusing to apologize. And the people who refused actually felt more powerful afterwards. For a while, they even felt a little better about themselves. Refusing to say sorry can feel like protecting yourself. It feels strong in the moment. Now you know why they do it. But that good feeling doesn't fix anything for the other person. And it can quietly cost you the relationship. And most of us find it much easier to trust someone who can admit a mistake. So if apologies are this powerful, why do so many of them still fall flat? A group of researchers wanted to find out which parts of an apology matter most. They broke a good apology into six parts. Saying you're sorry. Explaining what happened. Admitting it was your fault. Saying it won't happen again. Offering to fix it. And asking to be forgiven. Then they asked hundreds of people to rate apologies with different parts missing. The most important part, by far, was admitting it was your fault. "I'm sorry you feel that way" quietly tells the other person they're the problem. The real thing is four words. "I got that wrong." The second most important part was offering to fix it. And the part that mattered least? Asking to be forgiven. So the strongest apologies are about the other person, not about getting yourself off the hook. Imagine you forgot a friend's birthday. "Sorry, I've just been so busy," is a weak apology. "I forgot your birthday, and that's on me. Can I take you to dinner this week?" is a strong one. But it wasn't over yet. Because when you apologize matters too. Researchers asked people to think about a time someone had upset them. Some imagined getting an apology straight away. Others imagined getting it later, after they had a chance to talk. The later apologies landed better. Waiting on its own didn't do the trick. What made the difference was that, by then, the person felt properly heard. A rushed "sorry" can feel like a way to close the conversation. A rushed sorry is about ending your own discomfort. Think about how it feels when someone apologizes while you're still in the middle of explaining. It can make you more annoyed, not less. A sorry that comes after listening feels like you actually understood. So the next time you need to apologize, try listening first. One honest sentence after listening is worth more than ten quick sorries. There's one more apology most of us forget. The one we owe ourselves. In another study, people thought about something they regretted doing. Some were asked to write to themselves kindly, the way a good friend would. Those people were more motivated to make amends, and to avoid doing it again. They weren't letting themselves off the hook. They were giving themselves the energy to do better. Being harsh with yourself can feel responsible. If you're someone who says sorry for everything, you're probably also very good at being hard on yourself. But kindness seems to make it easier to actually do better. Next time you replay a mistake at night, talk to yourself like a friend who made it. So how can you use this in your own life? Here are five simple rules. Rule one. Stop apologizing for taking up space. Save "sorry" for when you've actually done something. If you're just asking a question, try "excuse me," or simply ask it. Rule two. Try swapping sorry for thank you. Instead of "Sorry I'm late," try "Thank you for waiting." It still shows you care, without making you smaller. It puts the focus on their kindness, instead of on your mistake. Rule three. When you do apologize, say what you did, and how you'll fix it. Rule four. Let the other person speak first. Then apologize. Ask them how it felt for them, and really listen to the answer. Rule five. After you've apologized, forgive yourself too. You can't take it back. You can only do better next time. So here's the twist. People who say sorry a lot are usually people who care a lot about others. That's a good thing. The goal is to make your sorry mean something again. But say sorry forty times a day, and the one that really counts gets lost in the crowd. Your sorry is powerful. Save it for the moments that matter, and say it like you mean it. And the next time you bump into a chair, you have my permission to say nothing at all. Here's your challenge for this week. Every time you're about to say "sorry" for something small, swap it for "thank you." Then notice how people react, and how you feel. Most people won't even notice the swap. But you will.

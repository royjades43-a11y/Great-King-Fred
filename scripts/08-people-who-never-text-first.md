# Video 8: The Psychology of People Who Never Text First

**Title:** The Psychology of People Who Never Text First
**Length:** about 8 minutes at the channel's pace; over 8:00 so mid-roll ads are possible

**How it differs from competitors:** most videos on this topic either call people who never text first
"low effort" or diagnose them ("avoidant attachment"). This one uses experiments with real numbers to show
that they usually care a lot. They're just wrong about how a message will land, and the friend they're
waiting for is often waiting for them.

**Engagement design:** opens on the viewer (the typed-and-deleted message); an open loop ("why the friend
you're waiting for is probably waiting for you") paid off in the twist; three "stories your brain tells
you", numbered; a running thread (the empty chat) that opens and closes the video; a callback to video 4
(the liking gap); every section ends on a hook.

Lines in *[brackets]* are visual notes for the edit; they are not read out.

---

## [0:00–0:40] HOOK

You open a chat with an old friend.
*[a phone screen; a chat with the last message dated 4 months ago]*
The last message is from four months ago.
You type, "Hey, how've you been?" Then you delete it.
*[the typed message disappears letter by letter]*
They're probably busy.
It's been too long.
It would be weird.
And anyway, if they wanted to talk, they'd text you.
Sound familiar?
Here's the strange part.
On the other side of that chat, your friend may be doing the exact same thing.
*[split screen: two stick figures, each holding a phone, each typing and deleting]*
Psychologists have now tested what happens when people send that message.
Across thousands of people, the senders got it wrong in the same direction, almost every time.
By the end of this video, you'll know why.
And why the friend you're waiting for is probably waiting for you.

## [0:40–1:55] THE REACH-OUT EXPERIMENTS

In twenty twenty-two, a team led by psychologist Peggy Liu ran a series of experiments on one simple act.
Reaching out.
More than five thousand nine hundred people took part, across thirteen experiments.
*[label: STUDY · Liu, Rim, Min & Min · 5,900+ people · 13 experiments]*
In the first, people remembered reaching out to someone, or someone reaching out to them.
People who remembered being contacted rated their appreciation higher than the senders imagined.
In others, people really did it.
They sent a short note to someone they hadn't talked to in a while.
Sometimes with a small gift, like cookies.
*[a note and a small box of cookies flying across]*
Then both sides answered the same question.
How much did the person appreciate it?
The senders guessed.
The receivers told the truth.
And the senders underestimated it.
*[two bars: SENDERS' GUESS · ACTUAL APPRECIATION, the second taller]*
Not once.
Again and again, across the studies.
People liked being reached out to far more than the person reaching out expected.
Here's the part that matters most.
The gap got bigger when the message was more surprising.
*[the gap between the bars widens]*
When it came from someone they weren't close to anymore.
When it arrived out of the blue, for no reason at all.
Think about that.
The thing that stops you from texting is "it's been too long." And that is exactly what makes the message land.
The weirdness you're afraid of is the best part of the gift.
So if a text means that much, why don't we send it?

## [1:55–3:55] THREE STORIES YOUR BRAIN TELLS YOU

Because when you look at that empty chat, your brain tells you three stories.
And all three are wrong.

Story number one: "They don't want to hear from me." If you've watched our video on small talk, you'll know the liking gap.
*[label: THE LIKING GAP · Boothby et al., 2018 (callback to video 4)]*
After a conversation, people consistently underestimate how much the other person liked them.
Even dormmates who'd lived together for months.
We carry that same doubt into the chat.
We remember every awkward pause.
We forget the part where they laughed.
And so we assume the silence means they've moved on.
Very often, it means they're busy.
Or they're waiting for you.
A friend who hasn't texted in months isn't a verdict.
It's a gap.

Story number two: "It'll be awkward." In twenty eighteen, psychologists Amit Kumar and Nicholas Epley ran a simple test.
People wrote a thank-you letter to someone who'd helped them.
*[label: STUDY · Kumar & Epley, 2018; a letter with a heart]*
Before sending it, the writers predicted how the receiver would feel.
They expected the letter to feel more awkward than it did.
And they underestimated how happy it would make the person.
Here's the human detail.
The writers worried about getting the words right.
The receivers barely noticed the words.
They noticed the warmth.
You're editing a sentence.
*[split: a stick figure frowning over a draft; another smiling at a letter]*
They're reading a feeling.
So when did you last delete a message because it didn't sound perfect?

Story number three: "If they cared, they'd text first." This one feels like self-respect.
You texted last time.
It's their turn.
Psychologists Margaret Clark and Judson Mills described two kinds of relationships.
*[two columns: EXCHANGE (a receipt) · COMMUNAL (a heart)]*
In exchange relationships, people keep score.
You did something for me, so I owe you.
That's how business works.
In communal relationships, like friendships, people give because they care, not to settle a debt.
Their research suggests that keeping a strict score is a sign of the first kind.
So counting who texted last quietly turns a friend into a transaction.
And here's the irony.
Your friend may be keeping the same score.
Two people, both waiting for the other to prove they care.
*[two stick figures, each staring at a silent phone]*
Both feeling a little forgotten.
Neither one forgot.

## [3:55–5:55] WHAT IT COSTS

Everyone knows this line.
"We should catch up soon!" "Definitely!" And then nobody does.
Not because anyone stopped caring.
Because both people assumed the other one would plan it.
So what happens if nobody goes first?
It isn't dramatic.
That's the problem.
Friendships rarely end with a fight.
They end with a pause that gets longer.
In twenty eighteen, communication researcher Jeffrey Hall estimated how much time friendship takes.
About fifty hours to go from acquaintance to casual friend.
*[a bar filling: 50 h · 90 h · 200+ h; label: Hall, 2018]*
About ninety hours to become a real friend.
And more than two hundred hours to become a close friend.
Those hours don't happen on their own.
Someone has to start them.
Psychologist Robin Dunbar's research suggests our friendships sit in layers.
*[concentric circles: 5 · 15 · 50 · 150]*
About five people in the innermost circle.
About fifteen in the next.
And those inner layers need regular contact to stay there.
Roughly weekly for the closest five.
Roughly monthly for the next fifteen.
Friends don't usually leave.
They slide outward, one layer at a time.
*[a stick figure drifting from the inner circle to an outer one]*
Dunbar and his colleague Sam Roberts followed students as they moved away to university, for eighteen months.
When contact with old friends dropped, the friendships faded.
Faster than family ties did.
And the friendships that survived were the ones where someone made an effort.
For the women in the study, talking more on the phone helped.
For the men, doing things together did.
A lot of people are now feeling the cost.
In a twenty twenty-one American survey, twelve percent of adults said they had no close friends at all.
*[two bars: 1990 3% · 2021 12%; label: American Perspectives Survey, 2021]*
In nineteen ninety, it was three percent.
Among men in that survey, it was fifteen percent.
And this isn't only about feelings.
A review of one hundred and forty-eight studies, covering more than three hundred thousand people, found something striking.
*[label: Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010 · 148 studies]*
People with stronger social ties had about fifty percent higher odds of survival over the study periods.
In twenty twenty-three, America's Surgeon General compared the health risk of disconnection to smoking up to fifteen cigarettes a day.
*[a cigarette icon with "up to 15 a day"]*
All of that can start with one message nobody sent.
But it wasn't over yet.

## [5:55–7:15] HOW TO TEXT FIRST

Remember what the receivers noticed that the senders missed.
Not the gift.
Not the wording.
The surprise.
The simple fact that someone thought of them, when they didn't have to.
That's what the friend you're waiting for wants too.
Not a perfect message.
Proof that they crossed your mind.
So how do you actually do it?

Rule one: don't wait for a reason.
*[a checklist builds, one rule per line]*
"Saw this and thought of you" is enough.
Out of the blue is a feature, not a bug.

Rule two: skip the apology.
You don't need to explain the four months.
"It's been forever, I miss talking to you" works better than a paragraph about how busy you've been.

Rule three: say something real.
A meme is a start.
But tell them one true thing, or ask one real question.
Instead of "how are you?", try "what's been the best part of your month?" Research on conversations suggests people want more depth than we expect.

Rule four: stop keeping score.
If you're the one who usually texts first, that doesn't make you needy.
It makes you the friend who keeps the friendship alive.

Rule five: make it a habit, not a mood.
Pick one person a week.
*[a calendar with one message icon each week]*
One message.
It takes about ten seconds.
Those ten seconds add up to the hours that friendship needs.

And the best part?
The research suggests the message doesn't have to be perfect.
It just has to be sent.

## [7:15–8:05] PUNCHLINE & CTA

So here's the twist.
People who never text first usually aren't cold.
Very often, they care a lot.
They're just afraid of being the one who cares more.
But the research says the person on the other side of that chat is probably not annoyed.
They're probably not busy forever.
And they'd probably love to hear from you.
They might be staring at the same empty chat, waiting for you.
*[the split screen from the hook; one figure presses send, the other's phone lights up]*
Someone has to go first.
Why not you?

So here's your challenge for today.
Think of one person you've lost touch with.
Send them one message.
No reason needed.
Then come back and tell me what they said.

Are you the friend who always texts first, or the one who waits?
Tell me in the comments.

Drop it in the comments.

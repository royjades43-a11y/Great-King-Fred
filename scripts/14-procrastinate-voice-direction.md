# Voice direction: "The Psychology of People Who Procrastinate"

A line-by-line reading guide, in the same format as videos 5 to 13 (see `scripts/05-always-late-voice-direction.md`
for the 12 rules and the full legend). It works for a human narrator, ElevenLabs or AI Studio (use each
section's **Direction** line as the style prompt), and for our free AI voice through `voice/directed.py`.

| Mark | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **word** | stress it |
| `\|` / `\|\|` | short / long beat |
| ↘ / ↗ | land down / lift (a genuine question) |
| (brisk) (slower) (land it) (lean in) (warm) (smile) (light) (character) | how to deliver the line |

**Written to be spoken, like one friend talking to another** (owner's rule from video 13). Use whole, natural
sentences with few marks, and make every line speak to the listener. Pause naturally at full stops and lift genuine
questions.

Numbers are written the way they are spoken.

## 1 · Hook (0:00–1:15)
**Direction:** light and knowing, like you're both guilty of it. "Octopuses" gets a small smile. The promise is clear and warm, spoken straight to the viewer. Then curious for the research. Energy 3, rising to 4 by the promise.

You sit down to finally do the thing you've been putting off. ||
Ten minutes later, the kitchen is clean. ||
The junk drawer is organized. And somehow, you're watching a video about how octopuses sleep. ||
The thing you sat down to do is still there, waiting for you. ||
If that sounds familiar, you're in very good company. ||
And if you're watching this instead of doing something else right now, don't worry. I won't tell anyone. ||
So in this video, we're going to look at why your brain keeps putting things off. ||
You'll find out why your brain treats future you like a complete stranger. ||
And by the end, you'll know the one small habit that can break the cycle. ||
Let's start with how common this really is. ||
In two thousand seven, psychologist Piers Steel pulled together hundreds of studies on procrastination. ||
He estimated that up to one in five adults put things off chronically. ||
Among college students, it was most of them. ||
So why do we do it, even when we know better? ||
Most of us grew up believing it comes down to laziness, or bad time management. ||
Psychologists Fuschia Sirois and Tim Pychyl see it differently. ||
Their research suggests procrastination is mostly about how a task makes you feel. ||
Maybe it's boring. Maybe it's confusing. Maybe you're worried you'll do it badly. ||
Whatever the feeling is, putting the task off makes it go away. For a little while. ||
And your brain remembers that relief. So next time, it reaches for it even faster. ||
Have you ever noticed that the things you avoid the most are often the ones you care about the most? ||

## 2 · The semester (1:15–2:25)
**Direction:** a story you're enjoying telling. "Looked like the winners" with a knowing smile. "Then the deadlines arrived." gets a beat before it. "The very long night" lands warm and a little funny. Energy 3.

Here's where it gets interesting. ||
In nineteen ninety-seven, psychologists Dianne Tice and Roy Baumeister followed a class of college students through a whole semester. ||
They measured who procrastinated, and they kept track of everyone's stress and health. ||
Early in the semester, the procrastinators looked like the winners. ||
They were less stressed than everyone else. They even reported fewer signs of illness. ||
They were the relaxed ones, while everybody else was already working. ||
Then the deadlines arrived. ||
By the end of the semester, the procrastinators were more stressed, and they were getting sick more often. ||
And their grades on the papers were lower. ||
Added up over the whole semester, the procrastinators actually ended up with more stress and more illness. ||
So the relief was real. It was just borrowed, and it came back with interest. ||
You've probably felt this yourself. The calm week, and then the very long night. ||

## 3 · Future you (2:25–4:00)
**Direction:** curious, then playful. "Picture the taste for a second" is a grin. "Future you gets the ketchup" is the line people will quote: land it, then pause. The aged-photo study is hopeful. Energy 4.

So why doesn't that long night stop us the next time? ||
Part of the answer is how your brain thinks about future you. ||
In two thousand nine, psychologist Hal Hershfield and his colleagues scanned people's brains while they thought about different people. ||
Sometimes they thought about themselves right now. Sometimes they thought about themselves ten years from now. ||
And sometimes they thought about strangers. In this study, the strangers were famous actors. ||
For a lot of people, the brain responded to future self much like it responded to the strangers. ||
In other words, future you can feel like somebody else. ||
And that changes how you treat them. ||
A year earlier, psychologist Emily Pronin and her colleagues tested this with a disgusting drink. ||
It was a mix of ketchup and soy sauce. Picture the taste for a second. ||
People had to decide how much of it would be drunk, all in the name of science. ||
When they chose for themselves, right now, they picked a small amount. ||
When they chose for their future selves, they signed up to drink a lot more. ||
About as much as they'd hand to a stranger. ||
That's procrastination in one cup. Present you gets the break. Future you gets the ketchup. ||
Later, Hershfield tried something more hopeful. ||
He showed people realistic, aged-up pictures of their own faces. ||
After meeting their older selves, they chose to save more than twice as much for retirement. ||
When future you feels real, you start looking after them. ||

## 4 · I'll start Monday (4:00–5:00)
**Direction:** warm and a little guilty, like confessing together. The question is sincere, almost one-to-one. "Where things go to wait" is soft. Energy 3.

Now let me ask you something. How many times have you said, "I'll start on Monday"? ||
You're not alone. Psychologists have actually measured it. ||
In twenty fourteen, Hengchen Dai, Katherine Milkman and Jason Riis looked at Google searches for the word "diet." ||
Searches jumped at the start of every week, every month, and every new year. ||
Visits to the gym rose at those same moments too. ||
They called it the fresh start effect. ||
Maybe you've got a list somewhere right now, with a plan that starts on Monday. ||
A new week feels like a clean page. The old you made the mess. The new you will fix it. ||
Here's the irony. That same feeling is what lets us push things back in the first place. ||
Monday can be a real starting line. Or it can be the place where things go to wait. ||
The difference is what you do when Monday actually comes. ||

## 5 · Deadlines (5:00–6:00)
**Direction:** brisk and clear, like a fun experiment. "They landed in the middle" is a small reveal. Then a real pause before "But it wasn't over yet.", low and close. Energy 4.

So what about deadlines? Do they actually help? ||
In two thousand two, Dan Ariely and Klaus Wertenbroch tested this with people who were paid to proofread. ||
Some had one deadline at the very end. Others had to hand in a section every week. ||
A third group got to choose their own deadlines. ||
The people with weekly deadlines found the most errors. They were late the least, and they earned the most. ||
The people with one big deadline at the end did the worst. ||
And the people who chose their own deadlines? They landed in the middle. ||
Most of them knew they needed deadlines. They just didn't set them tight enough. ||
We all know ourselves a little. Just not quite well enough. ||
But it wasn't over yet. ||

## 6 · The one habit (6:00–7:20)
**Direction:** the payoff of the promise, warm and bright. Slow down on "procrastinated less the second time around." The question to the viewer is gentle. Energy 4.

Because the most useful finding about procrastination is also the kindest one. ||
In twenty ten, Michael Wohl, Tim Pychyl and Shannon Bennett followed first-year students through their exams. ||
Before the first exam, plenty of them put off studying. ||
Afterwards, the researchers asked how much they had forgiven themselves for it. ||
Then they waited for the second exam. ||
The students who had forgiven themselves procrastinated less the second time around. ||
The students who were still beating themselves up procrastinated more. ||
Notice that nobody told them to try harder. Nobody handed them a new planner. ||
What helped was a little kindness. ||
So why would being kind to yourself help you get more done? ||
Think back to the start of this video. Putting things off is a way of escaping a bad feeling. ||
Guilt is a bad feeling too. So the harder you are on yourself, the more there is to escape. ||
Forgiving yourself takes some of that weight off the task. ||
And suddenly, starting it doesn't feel quite so heavy. ||
Would you call a friend a failure for putting off one assignment? Probably not. ||
You'd tell them to let it go, and start again. ||

## 7 · Five rules (7:20–8:30)
**Direction:** friendly and practical, like a friend sharing what actually works. Each "Rule" is a small headline. Energy 3.

So how can you use this in your own life? Here are five simple rules. ||
Rule one. Name the feeling. Ask yourself, "What am I trying not to feel right now?" ||
Bored, confused, or scared of doing it badly. Once you know, it loses some of its grip. ||
Rule two. Make the first step tiny. Not "write the report." Just "open the document." ||
Rule three. Turn your plan into an "if, then." If it's nine in the morning, then I open the document. ||
Psychologists Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran reviewed ninety-four tests of plans like these. ||
On average, people who made them were more likely to follow through. ||
It works because the decision is already made, before the moment arrives. ||
Rule four. Set small deadlines, and tell someone about them. One big deadline is where things go to hide. ||
Rule five. Forgive yesterday. You can't change it. You can only start the next part. ||
And be patient with yourself. A habit that took years to build takes a little while to change. ||

## 8 · Twist and ending (8:30–9:20)
**Direction:** soft and generous, talking to one person. The junk-drawer callback is warm and a little funny. The challenge is bright. The question is sincere. Energy 2.

So here's the twist. ||
Most of us think people procrastinate because they don't care enough. ||
Very often, it's the opposite. We avoid the things that matter, because they make us feel something. ||
Your brain is trying to protect your mood. It's just a little too good at it. ||
So the next time you find yourself organizing the junk drawer, go easy on yourself. ||
Notice the feeling. Forgive the delay. Then take one tiny step. ||
Future you is going to be very grateful. ||
And hey, at least the kitchen got cleaned. ||
Here's your challenge for this week. ||
Pick one thing you've been putting off. Write down the very first step, as small as you can make it. ||
Then do just that one step, today. ||
And I'd love to know something. What's the one thing you've been putting off the longest? ||
Let me know in the comments. I promise I won't judge. ||
Which habit should we cover next? Drop it in the comments. ||

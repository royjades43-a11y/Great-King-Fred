It's late. You should be asleep. Instead, you're staring at one text. You wrote, "Sounds good! Can't wait!" They wrote back, "ok." No capital letter. No exclamation mark. Just "ok." Are they mad? Did you say something wrong? Was the exclamation mark too much? Forty minutes later, you're still thinking about it. And they're asleep. Sound familiar? You're not crazy, and you're not dramatic. Your brain thinks it's doing its job. It's trying to keep you safe by thinking it through one more time. And then one more time after that. Psychologists have studied what all that thinking actually does. They've pinged thousands of people to ask what's on their minds. They've made students wear an embarrassing T-shirt. And they've checked how many worries ever come true. By the end of this video, you'll know why your mind keeps replaying things. And the one simple move that helps it let go. Let's start with where your mind goes when you're not looking.

In two thousand ten, Harvard psychologists Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert built an iPhone app. It pinged people at random moments during their day. It asked three things. What are you doing? What are you thinking about? How do you feel? More than two thousand people answered. Their minds were somewhere else almost half the time. Forty-six point nine percent, to be exact. In the shower. At work. Even in the middle of a conversation. And when their minds wandered, people were less happy. Even pleasant daydreams didn't make them any happier than staying present. The researchers put it right in their title. "A wandering mind is an unhappy mind." For an overthinker, the mind doesn't just wander. It wanders back to the same spot. The text. The meeting. The thing you said at a party six years ago. So what's it looking for back there?

Usually, it's looking for one thing. What other people think of you. And here's the problem. You think they're thinking about you much more than they are. In two thousand, psychologist Thomas Gilovich and his colleagues ran a slightly cruel experiment. They had college students put on a T-shirt with Barry Manilow's face on it. For those students, that was deeply embarrassing. Then each student walked into a room full of their peers. Afterward, the wearers guessed that about half the room had noticed the shirt. In reality, only about a quarter had. Psychologists call this the spotlight effect. You feel like you're on stage. Everyone else is busy feeling like they're on stage too. And it doesn't stop at T-shirts. In two thousand eighteen, psychologist Erica Boothby and her colleagues paired up strangers for a chat. Afterward, each person guessed how much the other one liked them. Most people guessed too low. Their partners liked them more, and enjoyed the talk more, than they realized. The researchers called it the liking gap. Between new college roommates, it lasted for months. So that conversation you've been replaying all week? The other person probably walked away thinking it went fine.

But overthinking feels useful. It feels like you're working on the problem. Is it? Psychologists have a word for this. Rumination. It comes from the way a cow chews its cud. Chew, swallow, bring it back up, chew it again. In nineteen ninety-five, psychologists Sonja Lyubomirsky and Susan Nolen-Hoeksema tested whether it helps. They brought in people who were already feeling down. Some spent a few minutes focusing on their feelings, and why they felt that way. Others spent those minutes thinking about something else entirely. Then everyone tried to solve everyday problems with other people. The ruminators came up with worse solutions. They felt worse, too. The thinking felt like progress. It was actually a loop. And the things you're worried about? In twenty twenty, psychologists Lucas LaFreniere and Michelle Newman ran a simple test. People with anxiety wrote down their worries for ten days. Later, they checked which ones had actually come true. About ninety-one percent hadn't. For many people, not a single worry came true. Each worry felt like an emergency. Most were false alarms. So how much of your week have you spent on something that never happened?

Overthinking hits decisions hardest. And here, more thinking can lead to a worse choice. In nineteen ninety-three, psychologist Timothy Wilson and his colleagues let students pick a free poster to take home. Some just picked the one they liked. Others first wrote down why they liked or disliked each one. Weeks later, the researchers called them back. The students who had analyzed their choice were less happy with their poster. Writing reasons pushed them toward what was easy to explain, not what they really liked. In two thousand six, psychologists Sheena Iyengar, Rachael Wells, and Barry Schwartz followed students looking for their first jobs. Some were maximizers. They wanted the best possible option, not just a good one. The maximizers landed jobs paying about twenty percent more. And yet, they felt worse. They were less satisfied with the job they chose. They felt more stressed and anxious along the way. They did better, and felt worse. Schwartz had written a whole book about this, called The Paradox of Choice. More options, he argued, can leave us less satisfied. Ever spent forty minutes picking a show, and then watched nothing? But it wasn't over yet.

Because there's a reason your brain won't drop a thought. Unfinished business sticks. Your mind keeps tapping you on the shoulder about what isn't done. It's often called the Zeigarnik effect, after psychologist Bluma Zeigarnik. As the story goes, it started in a restaurant in the nineteen twenties. The waiters remembered every unpaid order. Once the bill was paid, the order vanished from their heads. Her experiments found that people recalled interrupted tasks better than finished ones. In two thousand eleven, psychologists E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister tested how to make it stop. They had people think about an important goal they hadn't finished yet. Then they gave them a passage to read. Their minds kept drifting back to the goal. Then some people did one small thing. They wrote a specific plan for it. When, where, and how. They didn't do the task. They just planned it. And the intrusive thoughts largely went away. Your brain doesn't need the problem solved tonight. It needs to know there's a plan. There's one more trick, and it sounds a little silly. In two thousand fourteen, psychologist Ethan Kross and his colleagues gave people five minutes to prepare a speech. Some talked to themselves as "I." Others used their own name. "Sam, you can do this." The name group felt less anxious. Judges rated their speeches better. And afterward, they spent less time beating themselves up. Using your name puts a little distance between you and the problem. It's like giving advice to a friend. Why is it so much easier to fix a friend's problem than your own?

So here's what you can do with all this. Rule one: turn the worry into a plan. When a thought keeps coming back, ask, "What's the next step?" Write it down, with a when and a where. Then let your brain go off duty. Rule two: keep a worry receipt. Write tonight's worry down. Check it in a week. Did it happen? Most of the time, the answer will be no. And your brain needs to see that in writing. Your worry has a terrible track record. Make it show its receipts. Rule three: talk to yourself by name. "Okay, Sam. What would you tell a friend right now?" It sounds strange. It works better than it sounds. Rule four: decide what's good enough. For small choices, set a bar before you start. The first option that clears it wins. A good dinner tonight beats a perfect dinner at ten at night. And rule five: give your mind a real job. Rumination loves an idle mind. Take a walk. Call someone. Cook something. Then come back to the problem, if it still needs you.

So here's the twist. Overthinkers aren't broken. Very often, they're the most careful people you know. They remember details. They think about how others feel. They see problems coming before anyone else does. The same mind that replays an awkward text remembers your best friend's coffee order. That's a real strength. Thinking isn't the problem. Thinking in circles is. So the next time your phone just says "ok," take a breath. They're probably just busy. Or asleep. And you can be too. So here's your challenge for this week.

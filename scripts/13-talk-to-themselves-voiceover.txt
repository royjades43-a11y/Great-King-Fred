Have you ever watched a four-year-old play with blocks? They talk the whole time. "This one goes here. No, not that one." And nobody thinks they're crazy. We think it's cute. But if you caught a grown adult doing the same thing, alone in the kitchen, you'd probably wonder. Maybe you've been that adult. Most of us have. So in this video, we're going to look at why your brain talks to itself. You'll find out why some people don't hear an inner voice at all. And by the end, you'll know the one small word that can change how your self-talk works. Let's go back to that four-year-old. Back in the nineteen thirties, a psychologist named Lev Vygotsky spent a lot of time watching children like this. Most adults would call all that chatter babbling. Vygotsky saw something different. He believed those children were actually thinking, just out loud. His team even made the tasks harder on purpose. Imagine a child drawing, and the blue pencil they need has quietly gone missing. When that happened, the children talked to themselves even more. Vygotsky believed that as we grow up, that voice slowly moves inside. It becomes the quiet voice you might be hearing in your head right now. But it never fully goes away. When you're under pressure, it tends to come back out. Have you ever caught yourself saying, "Okay, okay, focus," right before something hard?

You've lost your keys. You're walking around the kitchen, and you hear yourself saying, "Keys. Keys. Where are my keys?" Then someone walks in, and you suddenly feel a little bit crazy. Most of us do this, and just hope nobody's listening. That's your inner four-year-old, still trying to help. And here's the interesting part. Saying "keys" out loud might actually help you find them. In twenty twelve, psychologists Gary Lupyan and Daniel Swingley put this to the test. They asked people to find everyday objects among a crowd of pictures. Some of them said the object's name out loud while they searched. And those people found the object faster. So the habit that makes you feel a bit silly might be making you sharper. Lupyan explained it with a banana. When you say "banana," your brain calls up what a banana looks like. Yellow, curved, easy to spot. There is one catch. The word only helps if your brain already knows what it's looking for.

So how often does that inner voice of yours actually talk? A psychologist named Russell Hurlburt has spent decades trying to find out. Starting in the nineteen seventies, he gave people a beeper that went off at random moments in their day. Every time it beeped, they'd write down exactly what was going on in their mind. If you're like most people, you'd guess your inner voice runs all day long. It speaks up a lot less than that. In one study, inner speech showed up in fewer than a third of those moments. The rest of the time, people were seeing pictures, or feeling something with no words at all. Here's the funny part. On questionnaires, people report two to four times more inner speech than the beeper finds. So we're not even very good at knowing what goes on in our own heads. And the differences between people were huge. Some people talked to themselves almost constantly. Others hardly ever did. What about the people whose inner voice is never there at all?

In twenty twenty-four, psychologists Johanne Nedergaard and Gary Lupyan decided to study those people. Yes, the same Gary Lupyan from the keys experiment. They gave the experience a name. Anendophasia. It simply means living without an inner voice. In twenty twenty, a blog post about it went viral. Thousands of people were stunned to learn that not everyone has an inner monologue. And plenty of others were just as stunned to learn that anyone does. Nedergaard and Lupyan wanted to know whether it actually changes how people think. So they compared adults with very little inner speech to adults with a lot of it. In one, people looked at two pictures and decided whether their names rhymed. Think of a sock and a clock. In another, they had to remember a short list of words. On both of those, the people with little inner voice did worse. But on the other tasks, the two groups did just as well as each other. How common is a quiet mind? Honestly, nobody knows yet. So let me ask you something. When you read a message from a friend, do you hear it in a voice? If you don't, there's nothing wrong with you. Your brain simply does things its own way.

Athletes figured this out long before scientists did. Tennis players mutter to themselves between points. Runners whisper "keep going" in the last stretch of a race. In twenty eleven, sport psychologist Antonis Hatzigeorgiadis and his colleagues pulled together thirty-two studies. Does talking to yourself actually improve performance? The answer was yes. The effect was moderate, but it was real. And it worked best in two situations. First, when a skill needed precision, like sinking a golf putt. And second, when the task was new to you. Short reminders about technique helped the most. Things like "eyes on the ball," or "elbow up." Here's the irony. The best players in the world do this in front of millions of people. And most of us still feel embarrassed doing it alone in our own kitchen. But it wasn't over yet.

Because the way you talk to yourself might matter even more than whether you do. And it can come down to one tiny word. The word "I." In twenty fourteen, psychologist Ethan Kross and his colleagues gave people five minutes to prepare a speech. Some of them talked to themselves using "I." Others used their own name, like, "Sam, you can do this." The people who used their name felt less anxious. And judges rated their speeches as better. You might remember a famous example of this. In twenty ten, basketball star LeBron James explained a huge career decision. He said he wanted to do what was best for LeBron James. A lot of people laughed. Psychologists recognized exactly what he was doing. Three years after the speech study, Kross and psychologist Jason Moser looked inside the brain. People looked at upsetting pictures while talking to themselves. When they used their name, the brain's emotional reaction dropped within about a second. And it didn't take any extra effort. The same year as the speech study, psychologists Sanda Dolcos and Dolores Albarracín tried the word "you." People who told themselves "you can do it" solved more word puzzles than people who said "I can do it." So why would one small word make such a difference? When you use your name, or "you," you step back from the problem a little. You start coaching yourself, the same way you'd coach a friend. And isn't it strange how much kinder we are to our friends than we are to ourselves?

So how can you use this in your own life? Here are five simple rules. Rule one. When you're looking for something, say it out loud. Keys. Phone. Wallet. It works best when you already know what the thing looks like. Rule two. When you're stressed, switch from "I" to your own name. Or try "you." It takes a second, and it costs you nothing. Rule three. When you're learning a skill, keep your self-talk short. Two or three words about technique, like "slow down," or "breathe out." Practice those words when you're calm, so they're ready when you're not. Rule four. Talk to yourself the way a good coach would. Would you call a friend an idiot for dropping a glass? Then try not to say it to yourself, either. Your brain is listening to every word. And rule five. Get curious about your own inner voice. Does it speak in full sentences? Does it even sound like you? And if yours turns out to be quiet, that's perfectly fine.

So here's the twist. Most of us grew up hearing that talking to yourself means you're losing it. The research points almost the other way. Self-talk is one of the oldest tools your brain has. You started using it when you were four, chatting away to your blocks. Somewhere along the way, most of us just learned to whisper. So maybe the better question is this. What are you saying when you talk to yourself? And if your inner voice is quiet, you've simply been solving problems your own way all along. So the next time you catch yourself muttering, "Keys, keys, keys," go easy on yourself. Your brain is just doing its job. Here's your challenge for this week.

You've lost your keys. You're walking around the kitchen, and you hear yourself say it. "Keys. Keys. Where are my keys?" Then someone walks in. And suddenly you feel a little bit crazy. Almost all of us do this. We just hope nobody hears. Here's the twist. Saying "keys" out loud may have helped you find them. In twenty twelve, psychologists Gary Lupyan and Daniel Swingley tested exactly that. People searched for everyday objects among a crowd of pictures. Some said the object's name out loud as they looked. Saying the name sped up the search. So the habit that makes you look strange might be making you sharper. By the end of this video, you'll know why your brain talks to itself. Why some people hear no inner voice at all. And the one small word that changes how your self-talk works. Let's start with why your brain needs words at all.

Watch a four-year-old play with blocks. They narrate everything. "This one goes here. No, not that one." In the nineteen thirties, psychologist Lev Vygotsky noticed this. He argued it isn't babbling. It's thinking, out loud. His team even tried making the tasks harder. Say, the blue pencil a child needed had quietly gone missing. The children talked to themselves even more. The harder the problem, the louder the thinking. As children grow, that voice slowly moves inside. It becomes the quiet voice in your head. But it never fully leaves. Under pressure, it comes back out. Ever caught yourself saying, "Okay, okay, focus," before something hard? That's the four-year-old, still at work. Words help your brain hold onto a plan. They point your attention at the right thing. Lupyan explained it with a banana. Say "banana," and your brain wakes up what bananas look like. Yellow. Curved. But there's a catch. The word only helps if your brain already knows what to look for. Words aren't magic. They're a tool.

So how often does your inner voice actually talk? Psychologist Russell Hurlburt spent decades finding out. He started back in the nineteen seventies, with a homemade beeper. He gave people that beeper, and it went off at random moments. Each time it beeped, they noted what was in their mind right then. Most people assume their inner voice runs all day. It doesn't. When people fill in a questionnaire, they report two to four times more inner speech than the beeper finds. We're not even good at knowing what goes on in our own heads. In one study, inner speech showed up in fewer than a third of the moments. Other times, people saw pictures. Or felt feelings, with no words at all. And the differences between people were huge. Some talked to themselves almost constantly. Others, almost never. Which raises a strange question. What if, for some people, the answer is never?

In twenty twenty-four, psychologists Johanne Nedergaard and Gary Lupyan studied those people. Yes, the same Gary Lupyan from the keys experiment. They gave it a name. Anendophasia. Life without an inner voice. A few years earlier, in twenty twenty, a blog post about this went viral. Thousands of people learned that not everyone has an inner monologue. Some were stunned. Others were stunned that anyone had one. Nedergaard and Lupyan wanted to know if it actually changes anything. They compared adults with very little inner speech to adults with a lot. Then they gave both groups a few puzzles. In one, people saw two pictures and decided if their names rhymed. A sock and a clock, for example. In another, they had to remember a short list of words. The people with little inner voice did worse on both. How common is a quiet mind? Nobody knows yet. People are remarkably poor at describing their own thoughts. But on other tasks, there was no difference at all. They weren't less smart. Their brains just took a different route. So here's a question for you. When you read a message, do you hear it in a voice? If you don't, you're not broken. Your brain just does it differently.

Athletes figured this out long before scientists did. Tennis players mutter between points. Runners whisper, "Keep going." In twenty eleven, sport psychologist Antonis Hatzigeorgiadis and his colleagues pulled together thirty-two studies. Their question was simple. Does self-talk actually improve performance? It does. The effect was moderate, but it was real. And it worked best in two situations. When a skill needed precision, like a golf putt. And when the task was new. Short cues helped with technique. "Eyes on the ball." "Elbow up." And it worked better when people had practiced their self-talk first. Like any skill, your inner coach gets better with training. The irony? The best players in the world do this in front of millions. And we still think it looks strange. So talking to yourself isn't weird. It's training. But it wasn't over yet.

Because how you talk to yourself matters as much as whether you do. And the difference comes down to one tiny word. "I." In twenty fourteen, psychologist Ethan Kross and his colleagues gave people five minutes to prepare a speech. Some talked to themselves as "I." Others used their own name. "Sam, you can do this." The name group felt less anxious. Judges rated their speeches better. You may have heard a famous version of this. In twenty ten, basketball star LeBron James explained a huge career decision. He said he wanted to do what was best for LeBron James. People laughed. Psychologists didn't. Three years later, Kross and psychologist Jason Moser looked inside the brain. People saw upsetting pictures, and talked to themselves either way. With their own name, the brain's emotional reaction dropped within a second. And it took no extra effort. That same year as the speech study, psychologists Sanda Dolcos and Dolores Albarracín tried "you." People who told themselves "you can do it" solved more word puzzles than people who said "I can do it." They also planned to work harder on the next ones. Why would a single word matter? Your name, or "you," puts a little distance between you and the problem. You stop drowning in it. You start coaching yourself, the way you'd coach a friend. Isn't it strange how much kinder we are to our friends than to ourselves?

So here's how to use your inner voice on purpose. Rule one: when you're searching, say it out loud. Keys. Phone. Wallet. Just make sure you know what you're looking for. The name only helps when your brain has a picture to match. Rule two: under stress, switch from "I" to your name. Or to "you." It takes a second, and it costs nothing. Try it on something small first. A tough email. A phone call you've been dreading. Rule three: for skills, keep it short. Two or three words about technique. "Slow down." "Breathe out." Practice them when things are calm, so they're ready when things aren't. Rule four: talk to yourself like a coach, not a critic. Would you call your friend an idiot for dropping a glass? Then don't say it to yourself. Your brain is listening to every word. And rule five: get curious about your own inner voice. Does it speak in full sentences? Does it sound like you? Some people find theirs is quiet. And that's fine, too.

So here's the twist. We grew up hearing that talking to yourself means you're losing it. The research says almost the opposite. It's one of the oldest tools your brain has. It started when you were four, narrating your blocks. Kids do it without any shame. Somewhere along the way, we just learned to whisper. And it never stopped working for you. The question was never whether you talk to yourself. It's what you say. And if your inner voice is quiet? Then you've been solving problems your own way all along. So the next time you catch yourself muttering, "Keys, keys, keys," don't feel crazy. You're just using your brain. So here's your challenge for this week. The next time you're nervous, talk to yourself by name. Out loud, if you can. Then notice what happens. And tell me something in the comments. Do you have an inner voice? Or is it quiet in there?

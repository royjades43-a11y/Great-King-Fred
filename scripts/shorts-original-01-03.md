the first two seconds, there's one idea per Short, and the last line works as a comment prompt. Each runs about
35–45 seconds (100–125 words).

## 01 · Your future best friend lives next door

**Hook on screen:** "Your best friend might live next door"

**Voiceover:**
```
Your future best friend might live next door. Literally. In nineteen fifty, psychologists studied a student housing block at MIT. Couples were given apartments more or less at random. Then the researchers asked who their friends were. People were about twice as likely to be friends with the neighbor next door as with someone two doors down. Not the funniest person. Not the most similar. The closest. Psychologists call it the propinquity effect. We like the people we bump into. The more you see a face, the more familiar it feels. And familiar feels safe. So if you want to get closer to someone, stop waiting for chemistry. Just keep showing up. Who's the person you see every day, but never talk to?
```

**Visual beats:**
- A corridor of stick-figure doors. Hearts and friendship lines link next-door neighbours, fewer two doors down, almost none at the far end.
- On "not the funniest, not the most similar", a joker stick and a twin stick get crossed out, and a red arrow points at the door next door.
- On "keep showing up", one stick passes the same neighbour three times: a nod, then a wave, then a chat bubble.

**Source:** Festinger, Schachter & Back (1950), *Social Pressures in Informal Groups* (the Westgate study). The
usual figures are about 41% of next-door neighbours named as friends vs about 22% two doors down; the script
says "about twice as likely".

## 02 · 36 questions that make strangers close

**Hook on screen:** "Strangers. 45 minutes. 36 questions."

**Voiceover:**
```
Can two strangers get close in forty-five minutes? In nineteen ninety-seven, psychologist Arthur Aron tried to find out. Yes, the same Arthur Aron from the famous bridge study. He paired up strangers and gave them thirty-six questions. They started small. Who would be your perfect dinner guest? Then they got deeper. What's your most treasured memory? When did you last cry in front of someone? Other pairs just made small talk for the same amount of time. The pairs with the questions felt much closer. The secret wasn't the questions themselves. It was taking turns opening up, a little more each time. Closeness isn't something you find. It's something you build, one answer at a time. Want to try it? Which question would you ask first?
```

**Visual beats:**
- Two stickmen sit at a table with a big "36" card between them and a stopwatch counting to 45:00.
- Each question pops up as a speech bubble, and a "closeness" meter between the two figures fills a step at a time.
- A second pair making small talk ("nice weather") has a meter that barely moves.
- On "taking turns", the two figures take turns leaning in.

**Source:** Aron, Melinat, Aron, Vallone & Bator (1997), *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*: "The
Experimental Generation of Interpersonal Closeness". The questions quoted are from the published list (shortened).

## 03 · Why you keep checking if they replied

**Hook on screen:** "Why you keep checking your phone"

**Voiceover:**
```
Why can't you stop checking if they've replied? Blame a pigeon. In the nineteen fifties, psychologist B. F. Skinner studied how pigeons learn to peck for food. When the food came on a steady schedule, the birds pecked at a steady pace. When it came unpredictably, they pecked like crazy. And they kept going long after the food stopped. Psychologists call it intermittent reinforcement. Unpredictable rewards are the hardest habits to quit. Now think about someone who replies in two minutes one day, and two days the next. Every check is a pull on a slot machine. That's not love growing. That's a reward schedule. The person who always replies might feel less exciting. But you'll never have to wonder. Who's your slot machine?
```

**Visual beats:**
- A stick pigeon at a button.
- On "steady", food drops on a regular beat and the pigeon pecks calmly.
- On "unpredictably", the food comes at random and the pigeon pecks frantically, with motion lines.
- The pigeon turns into a stickman tapping a phone. The phone screen becomes slot-machine reels, with "seen 2 min", then "seen 2 days".
- At the end, a calm stick holds a phone that shows a steady row of replies.

**Source:** Ferster & Skinner (1957), *Schedules of Reinforcement*: variable schedules produce high response
rates and the strongest resistance to extinction.

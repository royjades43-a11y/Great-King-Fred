You're lost. There's a person right there. You could just ask. Instead, you walk around the same block three times. Or you're buried at work, someone asks if you're okay, and you say, "I'm fine." Sound familiar? Here's the strange part. Psychologists have tested this, with thousands of strangers. And the people who won't ask are wrong about almost everything. Wrong about how many people will say yes. Wrong about how they'll feel. Wrong about how they'll see you. By the end of this video, you'll know why. And the one thing that makes people say yes. It isn't what you'd guess. Let's start with a man who figured this out almost three hundred years ago.

It's the story of one of history's most famous people, and one of his enemies. In the 1730s, Benjamin Franklin had a problem in the Pennsylvania Assembly. He'd just been chosen as its clerk, and he wanted to keep the job. A new member, wealthy and well-educated, made a long speech against him. Franklin didn't want to grovel. He didn't want to fight. So he did something strange. He heard that this rival owned a rare, unusual book. And he wrote him a note, asking if he could borrow it. The rival sent it straight away. Franklin returned it about a week later, with a note of thanks. The next time they met, the man spoke to him, for the first time, and with real courtesy. They became friends, and stayed friends until the man's death. Franklin wrote down the lesson in his autobiography. He called it an old saying he'd learned, now proven true. "He that has once done you a kindness will be more ready to do you another, than he whom you yourself have obliged." Asking didn't cost him anything. It won him a friend. So why do so many of us feel the exact opposite?

In 1969, two psychologists tested Franklin's idea. People took part in a contest and won some money. Afterwards, the researcher asked some of them for a favor. Could they give the money back? He'd been paying out of his own pocket. The people he asked ended up liking him more than the people he didn't. A favor didn't push them away. It pulled them closer. Then, in 2008, researchers at Stanford asked a simpler question. How many strangers do you think you'd need to ask, before one says yes? In one task, students had to get a stranger to walk them to the campus gym. They guessed they'd need to ask about seven people. In reality, it took just over two. In another study, volunteers raised money for charity. They expected to approach about two hundred and ten people to hit their goal. They needed about one hundred and twenty-two. And across studies with more than fourteen thousand strangers, people underestimated how often they'd hear yes by about half. Half. We think the world is full of people who'll say no. It's mostly full of people who'll say yes. And it goes further than you'd think. In one study, people asked strangers to do something a little bit wrong. Write the word "pickle," in pen, in a library book. Even then, more people agreed than the askers expected. If strangers will deface a library book for you, they'll probably hold a door. So why are we so sure they won't? Because when you're about to ask, your brain makes three predictions. And all three are wrong.

Many of us learned early that needing help means you didn't try hard enough. So we carry three fears into every ask. Fear number one: "They'll say no." Here's what your brain forgets. When you picture asking, you picture your embarrassment. You don't picture theirs. Saying no to a real person, face to face, is awkward too. Askers focus on how hard it is to ask. They forget how hard it is to refuse. That's why the yeses keep catching them off guard.

Fear number two: "I'll be a burden." In 2022, psychologists ran a set of experiments with more than two thousand people. In one, people asked strangers in a park to take their photo. The askers expected the helpers to feel inconvenienced. They were wrong. The helpers were more willing, felt better afterwards, and felt far less put out than the askers predicted. The team also asked people to remember times they'd helped someone else. Most of those memories were warm ones. Being asked had felt good. We think we're taking something from people. Often, we're giving them something. A small chance to be kind.

Fear number three: "They'll think I'm weak." This is the big one. In 2015, researchers at Harvard and Wharton tested it directly. In one study, about a hundred and seventy people solved brainteasers with a partner. Some partners asked for their advice. Some didn't. The people who were asked for advice rated their partner as more competent. Not less. More. Especially when the task was hard. And on easy tasks, asking didn't hurt at all. Being asked makes people feel smart. And they like the person who made them feel that way. There was one catch. It worked best when the person was asked directly, and when they actually knew the topic. So the fear of looking weak has it backwards. Think about the last time someone asked for your advice. Did you think less of them? Probably not. You probably felt a little flattered. So which fear is yours? Number one, two, or three? Put it in the comments.

Now think about what never asking really costs. The project that takes three days instead of one. The problem that grows while you wait. The question you never ask in class, so you stay confused for the rest of the term. The friend who would have helped in ten minutes, and never knew you needed it. And the slow, lonely feeling of carrying everything by yourself. Psychologist Nicholas Epley, who worked on that 2022 study, put it bluntly. Not asking, when someone would be happy to help, isn't kind. It's a little unkind. Because you're taking away their chance to help. It sounds harsh. But it wasn't over yet.

So what actually works? How do you ask? Rule one: ask in person. Not a text. Not an email. Walk over, or at least call. In 2017, researchers had people ask strangers to fill in a short survey. Some asked by email. Some asked face to face. Both groups expected about the same success. But the face-to-face requests were about thirty-four times more effective. And here's the twist inside the twist. When people ask by email, they do the opposite of everything we've seen. They overestimate the yeses. Later work found video and phone calls land somewhere in between. That's the thing I promised you. It isn't the perfect wording. It's showing up. An email is easy to ignore. A person standing in front of you is not. Rule two: be specific. "Could you look at this for ten minutes on Friday?" That's easy to say yes to. "Can you help me sometime?" That's easy to put off. Rule three: ask for advice, not just help. "What would you do?" makes the other person the expert. And as we just saw, that makes them think more of you, not less. Rule four: make "no" easy. Remember, saying no face to face is hard. So give people a real way out. "No worries if you can't" turns pressure into an invitation. The people who do say yes will mean it.

So here's the twist. People who never ask for help think they're protecting themselves. From the no. From being a burden. From looking weak. But the research says the no is rarer than you think. The burden is lighter than you think. And the weakness is in your head, not theirs. One of the most impressive people of his century didn't win over his rival by being impressive. He won him over by asking to borrow a book. Sometimes the strongest thing you can do is let someone help you. And the next time you're lost, just ask. Odds are, the second person you stop will walk you there.

# Voice direction: "The Psychology of People Who Are Always Late"

A line-by-line reading guide. It works for anyone recording the script: you, a voice actor, ElevenLabs
or Google AI Studio (paste the section's **Direction** line as the style prompt). It is also machine-readable:
`voice/directed.py` turns the marks below into pauses and pace changes for our free AI voice, which
is what fixes the "flat, evenly spaced" reading.

---

## The 12 rules behind every mark

These are the habits that separate a professional narrator from someone reading aloud.

1. **Talk to one person.** Picture the friend sitting alone at the restaurant table. Never "you guys",
   never a crowd. One person, across a small table.
2. **One operative word per thought.** Stress the *new* information, never the word you've already
   said. "They said thirty-four days. Their worst case was forty-nine. It took **fifty-five**."
3. **Land your periods.** Statements finish *down* (↘). Rising at the end of a statement ("upspeak")
   sounds unsure and makes facts sound like guesses.
4. **Real questions lift, rhetorical questions land.** "When will you finish?" lifts ↗ (we're asking
   the student). "So why don't we learn?" stays level, then a pause, so the viewer answers it in their head.
5. **Slow down for numbers.** Pause just *before* a number, stress it, and give the unit a hair of
   space: "about | **fifty-five** | days." Numbers are the payoff of this channel; never rush them.
6. **Make contrasts audible.** When two things are compared, stress *both* sides: "**Type A** … fifty-eight.
   **Type B** … seventy-seven."
7. **Lists of three build, then land.** Rise slightly through the first two, drop on the third:
   "One more email. One more episode. One more look for the ↘ **keys**."
8. **Pause before a reveal, pause after a punchline.** The setup gets a beat (`|`) so the viewer leans
   in; the punchline gets a longer beat (`||`) so it lands before the next line steps on it.
9. **Pace follows meaning.** Story: conversational. Data: slower. Jokes: light and quick, then a beat.
   The twist and the ending: slowest and lowest of the whole video.
10. **Let the smile be heard.** On warm or funny lines, literally smile while speaking. It brightens the
    tone without adding volume.
11. **Lean in for secrets.** "Here's the strange part" and "Think about that" are said a little quieter
    and closer, as if only to the friend at the table. Quieter, not slower and not breathier.
12. **Quotes get a light character.** When quoting someone ("Below average… but not by much"), shift
    the voice slightly: drier, smaller, a touch of the person. Don't do an accent.

Breathe at paragraph breaks and at `||`, never in the middle of a thought.

## Legend

| Mark | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **word** | operative word: stress it (a touch louder, longer, or higher) |
| `\|` | short beat, about a third of a second |
| `\|\|` | long beat, about three-quarters of a second: let it land |
| ↘ | land the line down (a statement, a punchline) |
| ↗ | lift (a genuine question) |
| (lean in) | quieter, closer, conspiratorial |
| (smile) | audible smile, warm and light |
| (brisk) | a little faster, energetic, moving the story on |
| (slower) | slow down for numbers or weight |
| (land it) | slowest delivery, lower pitch, full stop, then a long beat |
| (light) | quick and playful, a throwaway joke |
| (character) | a slight shift of voice for a quote |
| (warm) | gentle, kind, reassuring |

Numbers are written the way they are spoken ("nineteen fifty-seven"), so nobody has to translate digits
while reading.

## Energy map

```
energy  5 |  *
        4 |  * *         *                           *
        3 |      *   *       *   *   *   *       *       *
        2 |                                   *              *
        1 |                                                      *
          +--------------------------------------------------------
            hook  opera Kahneman data thesis lies  table  fix  twist end
```

The hook is the most curious, the restaurant table and the twist are the quietest, the fix is the
brightest after the hook. A flat energy line is what makes a narration sound "read".

## 1 · Hook (0:00–0:35)
**Direction:** intimate, knowing, a little amused. You're catching the viewer red-handed, kindly.
Pace: relaxed, with long beats. Energy 4.

(character) "I'll be there in **five** minutes." ||
(smile) You've sent that text. | Maybe **today**. ↘ ||
And twenty minutes later, | someone is still sitting alone at a table, | watching the **door**. ↘ ||
(lean in) Here's the **strange** part. |
You weren't **lying** when you sent it. |
You really **believed** five minutes. ||
(brisk) So did a **Nobel Prize** winner. | So did an entire **country**, | for ten years. ↘ ||
By the end of this video, you'll know why your brain gets **time** wrong. |
And the **one question** that fixes it. ||
(brisk) Let's find out where those missing fifteen minutes actually **go**. ||

## 2 · A whole country, ten years late (0:35–1:40)
**Direction:** storyteller. Build the scale, then let the Utzon detail be quietly sad. Pace: conversational,
slower on money. Energy 3–4.

(brisk) Let's start with the **country**. |
In nineteen fifty-seven, Sydney picked a design for a new **opera house**. |
The plan said it would be finished by nineteen sixty-three, | for about **seven million** dollars. |
(slower) It opened in nineteen **seventy**-three. ||
(land it) Ten years **late**. ||
And the final bill was about | **one hundred and two million** dollars. |
That's more than **fourteen times** the original price. ||
(warm) The architect, Jørn Utzon, | never even **saw** it finished. |
He quit in nineteen sixty-six, after a fight over money, | and went home to **Denmark**. |
(lean in) When the Queen opened his building in nineteen seventy-three, | he reportedly wasn't even **watching** on TV. ||
(brisk) You'd think that kind of disaster only happens to **giant** projects. |
(land it) It **doesn't**. ||
Because the man who **explained** it | was making the same mistake | at the very same **time**. ||

## 3 · The Nobel winner who was late (1:40–3:00)
**Direction:** a story with a twist inside it. Curious, then the expert's admission is awkward, dry, funny.
Pace: conversational; slow on "forty percent". Energy 3.

His name was Daniel **Kahneman**. |
In the early nineteen-seventies, he was on a team writing a new school **textbook** in Israel. |
After a year of work, he asked everyone a simple question. |
(character) How long until we're **done**? ↗ |
Everyone wrote down a number. | They all said about **two** years. ||
Then Kahneman turned to the team's curriculum expert | and asked something **different**. |
(character) How long did **other** teams like ours take? ↗ ||
(lean in) The expert went **quiet**. ||
Then he admitted it. |
(slower) About **forty percent** of those teams | never finished **at all**. |
The rest took **seven to ten** years. ||
So how did their **own** team compare? |
(character) "Below **average**," he said. | "But not by **much**." ||
(light) Nobody quit. | Nobody changed the plan. |
(land it) The book took **eight** years. ||
(smile) Kahneman later wrote that they should probably have quit that **day**. ||
In nineteen seventy-nine, Kahneman and Amos Tversky gave this mistake its name: | the **planning fallacy**. |
We plan for the **best-case** future. | Then the **real** one shows up. ↘ ||
That same year, the writer Douglas Hofstadter published a **joke** about it. |
(character) "It always takes longer than you expect, | even when you take into account Hofstadter's **Law**." ||
(smile) It's a joke. | It's also painfully **accurate**. |
(brisk) And the numbers back it **up**. ||

## 4 · Over budget, over time (3:00–3:40)
**Direction:** brisk and confident, like a quick montage. Numbers slower, everything else moving.
Energy 3.

(brisk) The economist Bent Flyvbjerg collected data on more than **sixteen thousand** big projects, | in over a hundred countries. |
(slower) Only about **eight and a half percent** | finished on time and on budget. |
Just **half a percent** also delivered everything they promised. ||
He calls it the **iron law**: | over budget, | over time, | over and over **again**. ↘ ||
(brisk) Boston's "Big Dig" tunnel was first budgeted at about **two point eight** billion dollars. |
It finished years late, in two thousand seven, | at about **fourteen point six** billion. ||
(light) Even airlines plan around it. | Many pad their schedules, so a slow flight can still land | "on **time**." ||
(smile) But maybe you're thinking: | that's governments and giant companies. | I'm just late for **brunch**. ||
(lean in) So let's shrink it down. | To one **student**, | and one **deadline**. ||

## 5 · Later than the worst case (3:40–5:00)
**Direction:** a slow-burn reveal. Set up the three numbers like a game show, then let the result hit.
Pace: slower overall. Energy 3, rising to 4 on "worse than the disaster".

In nineteen ninety-four, Canadian researchers asked thirty-seven psychology students about their final-year **thesis**. |
(character) When will you **finish**? ↗ | Give us your best guess. |
On average, they said about | **thirty-four** days. ||
Then the researchers asked a **sneakier** question. |
(character) What if everything goes as **badly** as it possibly could? ↗ |
Their worst-case guess was about | **forty-nine** days. ||
(lean in) So, | how long did it **really** take? ||
(land it) About | **fifty-five** days. ||
Later than their own **worst-case** scenario. |
(slower) Think about that. | They planned for **disaster**, | and reality was **worse** than the disaster. ||
Only about **thirty percent** finished by the day they'd predicted. |
And many of them **knew** they were usually late. | They just didn't think it would happen **this** time. ||
Researchers call that the **inside view**. |
You picture **this** task, | **this** week, | going smoothly. |
(light) You don't picture the printer jam, | the friend who calls, | or the bus that never **comes**. ||
So why don't we learn from the last **hundred** times? ||
(lean in) Because your brain has been quietly **lying** to you | about all of them. ||

## 6 · Three lies (5:00–6:30)
**Direction:** a numbered list with momentum. Each "Lie number…" is a fresh start, a little brighter.
The viewer test should sound like an invitation, with a smile. Energy 3–4.

(brisk) Lie number **one**: | your **memory**. |
In two thousand five, psychologists reviewed the research on how we predict **time**. |
We base our guesses on how long things took **before**. |
But we remember those past tasks as **shorter** than they really were. ||
Your morning routine might really take **forty** minutes. | Your memory might say **twenty-five**. |
So you plan tomorrow using the **wrong** number. | Every | single | **day**. ||
Two years later, the same researchers **tested** it. |
The people who remembered past tasks as **shortest** | underestimated the next one the **most**. ||

(brisk) Lie number **two**: | your sense of a **minute**. |
Researchers at San Diego State asked people to sit quietly | and guess when one minute had **passed**. |
Hurried "**Type A**" people called it at about | **fifty-eight** seconds. |
Relaxed "**Type B**" people waited until about | **seventy-seven** seconds. ||
That's almost **twenty seconds** of difference, | in a single minute. |
When one person thinks the minute is **up**, | the other still has twenty seconds to **go**. ||
(smile) Try it yourself. | Close your eyes, | count to what feels like one minute, | then check a **clock**. ||
Most people are **off**. | And now you know which way you **lean**. |
So "five minutes" **literally** doesn't mean the same thing to everyone. ||

(brisk) Lie number **three**: | (character) "I'll just do **one more thing**." |
(light) One more email. | One more episode. | One more look for the ↘ **keys**. |
Each one feels like it takes **no time** at all. |
(land it) Stack three of them, | and you're fifteen minutes late | before you've found your **shoes**. ||

And then there's **culture**. |
Psychologist Robert Levine measured the pace of life in **thirty-one** countries. |
He timed people walking, | timed post office clerks, | and checked the public **clocks**. |
**Switzerland** came out fastest, | with Japan and Western Europe close behind. |
In some places, five minutes late is **nothing**. | In others, | it's an **insult**. ||
(smile) So part of your clock was set | before you could even **read** one. ||

## 7 · The friend at the table (6:30–7:15)
**Direction:** the emotional low point. Quiet, slower, sincere, no judgement. Let the numbers sting
gently. Energy 2.

(lean in) Now go back to that friend at the **restaurant**. |
They don't see your memory bias, | your minute, | or your culture. |
They see someone who said | **five minutes**. ||
(slower) Ten minutes late, | three times a week, | adds up to about **twenty-six hours** a year. ||
(land it) A whole **day** of someone else's life. ||
And it feels even **longer** for them. |
In nineteen eighty-five, researcher David Maister wrote a famous paper on the psychology of **waiting**. |
His rule: | **empty** time feels longer than **busy** time. |
Your ten minutes **fly** by. | Theirs **crawl**. ||
(slower) It sounds hopeless. ||
(lean in) But it wasn't over **yet**. ||

## 8 · The fix (7:15–8:10)
**Direction:** brighter, practical, coach-like. Each fix is a clear step. The promised question is the
peak: slow it right down. Energy 4.

(brisk) So is a late person doomed to be late **forever**? | **No**. |
But here's what doesn't work: | trying **harder**. |
Kahneman's team heard the warning | and still took **eight** years. |
What works is changing how you **plan**. ||
(brisk) Fix **one**: | break it **down**. |
In two thousand four, a study found that listing every step | made people's time estimates longer | and less **optimistic**. |
The paper's title says it all: |
(character) "If you don't want to be late, | **enumerate**." ||
(light) Shower. | Get dressed. | Find keys. | Walk to the car. | Find **parking**. |
Every step you write down | is one your brain had **skipped**. ||
(brisk) Fix **two**: | plan **backwards**. |
A two thousand sixteen study found that working back from the deadline | made predictions less **optimistic**. |
Start with "I need to be in my seat at **nine**," | and walk it **back**. ||
(slower) And fix **three** | is the question I **promised** you. | The one Kahneman's expert answered. ||
(land it) Don't ask, | "How long **will** this take?" ||
(land it) Ask, | "How long **did** this take | **last** time?" ||
Then time your morning once, | with a **stopwatch**, | from alarm to front door. |
Most people are **shocked** by the number. | Plan with **that** one, | not the hopeful one. |
(smile) And add one "one more thing" on top, | because you **know** it's coming. ||
(light) Bonus trick: | put the time you need to **leave** in your calendar, | not the time you need to **arrive**. ||

## 9 · The twist and the ending (8:10–8:45)
**Direction:** the warmest and slowest part. Kind to late people, then the final line lands like a
quiet truth. CTA is relaxed and friendly, not salesy. Energy 2, then 3 for the CTA.

(slower) So here's the **twist**. ||
Many chronically late people | aren't careless at **all**. |
(warm) They're **optimists**. |
They really believe the **best** version of the day | is the one that's coming. |
(smile) Honestly, | as mistakes go, | that's a pretty **nice** one to make. ||
(warm) In nineteen ninety-nine, Sydney asked Utzon to come back | and help shape the building's **future**. |
In two thousand seven, his opera house became a **World Heritage** site. | He died the following year. ||
(slower) Today, | nobody remembers it was ten years **late**. ||
But your friend at the restaurant | will remember the **twenty minutes**. ||
(land it) Because **your** five minutes | and **their** five minutes | were never the **same** five minutes. ||

(smile) So, | are you the one who's always **late**, | or the one who's always **waiting**? ↗ | Tell me in the comments. |
Which habit should we cover **next**? ↗ | Drop it in the comments. ↘

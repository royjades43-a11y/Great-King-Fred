In 2014, psychologists in Chicago asked train commuters to do something most people would rather avoid. Talk to the stranger sitting next to them. Before they did it, people predicted it would make their ride worse. They were wrong. The talkers had a happier commute than the people who sat in silence. So why do so many of us hate small talk, when it keeps making us happier? By the end of this video, you'll know exactly what your brain is getting wrong.

The story starts on a tropical island, with a man who was technically an enemy of the state. His name was Bronisław Malinowski, a Polish anthropologist. When World War One broke out, he was in Australian territory, holding Austro-Hungarian papers. That made him an "enemy alien." Instead of locking him up, the authorities let him keep working. So he spent years in the Trobriand Islands, recording everything people did and said. And he noticed something odd. A lot of talk carried no real information at all. "How are you?" "Nice weather." "Where are you going?" In 1923, he gave it a name: phatic communion. It means bonding through words. The point of small talk, he argued, isn't what you say. It's that you said something at all. He also noticed what happened when nobody spoke. To most people, he wrote, a stranger's silence isn't reassuring. It's alarming. Small talk is how two people say, "I'm not a threat."

Why would humans need that? Look at our closest relatives. Monkeys and apes bond by grooming each other. Some species spend up to a fifth of their day doing it. It's slow, quiet and one-on-one. The psychologist Robin Dunbar argued that human groups got too big for that. You can't groom fifty friends. But you can talk to several of them at once. So, he suggested, conversation took grooming's job. And when his team listened in on real conversations, about two-thirds of the time went on social topics. Who did what. Who said what. How everyone's doing. In other words, the "pointless" talk was the point.

Now jump to 2014, and a coffee shop in Vancouver. Researchers stood outside a Starbucks and gave customers one of two instructions. Half were told to be as efficient as possible. Get in, pay, get out. The other half were told to smile, make eye contact, and have a short chat with the barista. The ones who chatted left in a better mood. And they felt a stronger sense of belonging. From a few seconds with someone whose name they didn't know.

And those small chats add up to more than a good mood. In 1973, sociologist Mark Granovetter interviewed people in a Boston suburb who had recently changed jobs. More than half had found their new job through a personal contact. But here's the strange part. Of those, about 84 percent got it through someone they saw only occasionally, or rarely. Not their best friends. Acquaintances. People they knew mostly through small talk. Why would that be? Close friends know what you know. Acquaintances know things you don't. Fifty years later, a study of about 20 million LinkedIn users pointed the same way. Moderately weak ties were the most useful for landing a new job. So that chat about the weather might be the start of your next career. So here's the question. If small talk is this old, and this good for us, why does it feel so awful?

Part of it is real. In 2010, psychologist Matthias Mehl had 79 students wear a small recorder for four days. Every twelve and a half minutes, it secretly recorded thirty seconds of their lives. Over those four days, it captured more than twenty thousand snippets of ordinary life. Then researchers sorted every conversation into small talk or something deeper. The happiest people had about twice as many deep conversations as the unhappiest. And only a third as much small talk. So the people who hate small talk are onto something. Depth seems to matter.

But here's where the brain starts lying. Back on those Chicago trains, commuters were asked a simple question. How many of the people around you would want to talk? They guessed fewer than half. In reality, the strangers were happy to talk. In one account, not a single person reported being snubbed. We assume everyone else wants to be left alone. Everyone else is assuming the same thing about us. So the whole carriage sits in silence, each person sure they're respecting everyone else's wishes.

Part of the problem is that we think we're being watched more than we are. In one famous experiment, students were asked to walk into a room wearing a T-shirt. The shirt had a huge picture of the singer Barry Manilow on it. The researchers picked him because, to these students, he was deeply uncool. The wearers guessed that about half the room had noticed the shirt. In reality, fewer than a quarter had. Psychologists call it the spotlight effect. In small talk, it makes every pause feel huge, and every clumsy line feel unforgettable. Most people don't notice. They're too busy worrying about their own shirt.

And then there's the silence itself. In 2011, Dutch researchers looked at what a short pause does to people in a conversation. A silence of about four seconds was enough to make people feel rejected. Four seconds. That's why small talk can feel like a test you're failing. Every pause feels like proof that the other person is bored of you. But they're probably feeling the exact same thing.

And it gets worse after the conversation ends. In 2018, researchers paired up strangers for a five-minute chat, then asked them a question. How much do you think your partner liked you? People consistently guessed too low. Their partners had liked them more, and enjoyed the chat more, than they realized. The researchers called it the liking gap. And it didn't vanish after one meeting. Among new college roommates, it lasted for months. The shyer the person, the bigger the gap. So when you walk away from small talk thinking "that was awkward," that feeling is often wrong. You just can't see it from the inside. But it wasn't over yet.

So what happens if you skip the small talk completely? In 2021, three researchers ran twelve experiments with more than 1,800 people. They paired strangers and gave them deep questions. Things like, "When did you last cry in front of another person?" Before talking, people expected it to be awkward. They expected the other person not to care. Then they talked. The conversations were far less awkward than predicted, and people felt more connected. They had also underestimated something else: how much the other person wanted to listen. When they tried both a shallow chat and a deep one, most preferred the deep one. The people who hate small talk were right about what they wanted. They were wrong about what other people wanted.

So how do you get from "nice weather" to something real? In 2017, Harvard researchers studied hundreds of live conversations between strangers. People who asked more questions were liked more. And one kind of question worked best: the follow-up question. "Wait, why did you move here?" "What was that like?" A follow-up shows you were actually listening. The people in the study didn't expect questions to make them more likeable. They did anyway. If you hate small talk, that's the shortcut. You don't need clever lines. You need one good follow-up.

So here's what the research adds up to. Assume people want to talk. Most of them do. Assume they like you more than you think. They usually do. And when there's a pause, ask one follow-up question instead of panicking. And next time someone asks "How are you?", try answering with one true thing. "Honestly? Tired, but good. I just started learning guitar." You've just handed them a follow-up question.

One last surprise. In 2018, a larger study of 486 people looked at small talk again. Deep conversations were still linked to happiness. But small talk itself wasn't linked to being unhappy. It didn't help or hurt. So small talk was never the enemy. It's the doorway. "Nice weather" is how two strangers check it's safe to say something real. The people who hate small talk don't hate people. They just want to skip the door. But you can't get into the room without walking through it.

# Video 3 (12-minute story version): Why We Rewatch the Same Shows

Lines in *[brackets]* are visual notes for the edit; they are not read out.

---

## [0:00–0:30] HOOK

In 2020, Americans spent 57 billion minutes watching one show.
It wasn't new. It was *The Office*, a sitcom that had ended seven years earlier.
*[counter spins up to 57,000,000,000 MINUTES]*
It's a show about a paper company. About people who are bored at work.
And millions of people picked it, again and again, over everything new on the planet.
By the end of this video, you'll know exactly why your brain keeps pressing replay.

## [0:30–1:30] ORIGINS

This story doesn't start with TV. It starts in 1688, with homesick soldiers.
A Swiss medical student named Johannes Hofer noticed something strange about Swiss soldiers serving abroad.
*[stick soldiers far from a mountain silhouette, slumped]*
They stopped eating. They couldn't sleep. Some grew so weak that doctors feared for their lives.
Hofer gave the condition a name: nostalgia.
It comes from two Greek words, "homecoming" and "pain."
He believed it was a disease of the brain.
Other doctors had their own theories.
Some blamed the endless clanging of cowbells in the Alps, which supposedly damaged the brain.
Others blamed the change in air pressure when people came down from the mountains.
The cures were rough. Leeches. Opium. Or a ticket back to the Swiss mountains.
Legend says one Swiss folk song was banned in the army because it made soldiers want to desert.
So for about two hundred years, missing the past was treated like an illness.
During the American Civil War, army doctors recorded thousands of soldiers with nostalgia as their diagnosis.
Some even listed it as a cause of death.
Remember that. Because today, it's one of the most valuable feelings on Earth.

## [1:30–4:00] RISE & GOLDEN AGE

Jump to 1951, Hollywood. A married couple is about to change television forever.
Lucille Ball and Desi Arnaz wanted to make a sitcom together.
The network, CBS, wanted it performed live in New York.
Desi said no. They would film it in Los Angeles, on real film, with three cameras.
*[three stick cameras swing in around a stage]*
That cost more money. So Desi made a deal.
His company, Desilu, would cover the extra cost. In return, Desilu would own the films.
CBS agreed. For CBS, it may be one of the worst deals in TV history.
Because film can be shown again. And again. And again.
*I Love Lucy* became the blueprint for the rerun.
And here's a human detail. When Lucille got pregnant, the network wouldn't let anyone say "pregnant" on air.
The scripts had to say "expecting" instead.
Then their real son was born on the same day the TV baby arrived on screen.
That night, about 44 million Americans watched Lucy's TV baby arrive, more than watched Eisenhower's inauguration the next day.

So why did people happily watch the same episodes twice?
In 1968, a psychologist named Robert Zajonc found a clue.
He showed people made-up words and strange symbols.
Some appeared once. Some appeared up to 25 times.
*[a nonsense word flashes 1×, 5×, 25×; a liking meter rises]*
The more often people saw something, the more they liked it. Even pure nonsense.
He called it the mere exposure effect.
Familiar feels safe. And safe feels good.

Now, what happens if you build an entire show around repetition?
In 1996, a kids' show called *Blue's Clues* tried exactly that.
It aired the same episode five days in a row. Monday to Friday.
Same dog. Same clues. Same answers.
Adults assumed kids would get bored. They didn't.
Researchers found that children understood more with each viewing, and joined in more by the end of the week.
*[a stick kid on the floor, shouting at the TV a little louder each day: MON… FRI]*

Then came the money.
*Friends* ended in 2004, and about 52 million Americans watched the final episode.
By then each of the six main actors earned about a million dollars per episode.
That's a lot to pay for one goodbye. But the goodbye never really happened.
Reruns kept the six friends in the same coffee shop for decades.
*Seinfeld*, a show famously "about nothing," has earned more than 3 billion dollars from reruns, by most estimates.
A show about nothing became one of the biggest money machines in TV.
So are we really paying for the jokes? Or for something deeper?

## [4:00–7:00] CONFLICT & COLLAPSE

To answer that, look at what changed in our lives.
In 2000, two researchers set up a jam-tasting table in a grocery store.
Some days the table offered 24 flavours. Other days, just 6.
*[a table fills with 24 jars, then shrinks to 6]*
The big table drew more shoppers. But only about 3 percent of them bought a jar.
At the small table, about 30 percent bought one.
When there were too many options, people froze.
Later studies found the effect isn't always that strong. But you've probably felt it.
Think about your streaming app. Thousands of titles. Endless rows of thumbnails.
How many nights have you scrolled for twenty minutes, then put on something you've seen ten times?
*[stick figure scrolling, eyes glazing; the thumb lands on the same old show]*
Netflix even interrupts you with a question: "Are you still watching?"
The honest answer is usually yes. For the fourth time this year.

Then there's loneliness.
In 2023, the US Surgeon General called loneliness an epidemic.
His report said about one in two American adults had experienced it.
And psychologists had already found a strange kind of relief.
In 2009, Jaye Derrick and her colleagues asked people to write about their favourite TV show.
Afterwards, people felt less lonely.
When people felt rejected, thinking about their favourite characters helped them recover.
*[label: SOCIAL SURROGACY · Derrick, Gabriel & Hugenberg, 2009]*
The researchers called it social surrogacy. Familiar characters work like stand-in friends.
Your brain knows the characters aren't real. It just doesn't seem to care very much.

So the comfort show became a shelter. Then the shelter began to crack.
In December 2018, fans learned *Friends* might leave Netflix.
Netflix reportedly paid around 100 million dollars to keep it for one more year.
For a show that had ended in 2004.
Then, on January 1st, 2020, *Friends* left Netflix in the US anyway.
*[the couch empties; the TV shows "NO LONGER AVAILABLE"]*
A few months later, the world shut down. Lockdowns. Fear. Nonstop news.
People needed their comfort shows more than ever.
And those shows were scattering across new apps, each with a new monthly price.
Comfort had become a product. And the product was getting expensive.
So what do you do when the world feels out of control?
You reach for the one thing that never changes.
Many people spent lockdown on the couch with characters they'd known for twenty years.
Not because they had nothing new to watch. Because new felt like too much.
But it wasn't over yet.

## [7:00–10:30] MODERN TRANSFORMATION

The streaming wars had begun. And the main weapons were old sitcoms.
WarnerMedia reportedly paid about 425 million dollars for five years of *Friends*.
NBCUniversal paid around 500 million to bring *The Office* to its own app.
*[money stacks drop beside each show: $425M · $500M]*
Half a billion dollars. For a show about selling paper.
In 2021, the *Friends* cast came back for a reunion special, sitting on a copy of the original set.
No new story. No new jokes. Just six older people in a familiar room, remembering.
Millions tuned in to watch people rewatch their own show.
Back in 2020, *The Office* was America's most-streamed show, at about 57 billion minutes.

So what are we actually buying? Psychology gives three answers.

The first is effort.
A new show asks a lot. New names, new faces, a story you have to care about from scratch.
A show you know asks for almost nothing.
Psychologists call this processing fluency. Things that are easy to take in feel good.
It's the same reason your favourite song feels better than a song you heard once.
After a long shift, your brain doesn't want a puzzle. It wants something it can follow with its eyes half closed.
*[label: PROCESSING FLUENCY]*

The second is control.
When life feels uncertain, a story with a known ending is a small safe room.
And knowing the ending doesn't spoil it.
In 2011, researchers at UC San Diego gave people a dozen short stories.
Some readers were told the ending first. They enjoyed the stories more, not less.
*[card: SPOILERS · Leavitt & Christenfeld, 2011]*
So the thing we all fear online, the spoiler, can make a story better.
Think about a kid asking for the same bedtime story every night.
They know how it ends. That's the point.
Knowing what comes next lets you relax and enjoy the ride, instead of bracing for it.

The third answer is nostalgia. Yes, Hofer's old disease.
Modern researchers like Constantine Sedikides and Tim Wildschut studied it properly.
Most people in their studies said they felt nostalgic at least once a week.
And it didn't make them sick.
Nostalgia made them feel more connected to others, and more sure their life had meaning.
*[card: NOSTALGIA · Wildschut, Sedikides et al., 2006]*
A feeling once treated with leeches turned out to be emotional first aid.
Which raises a fair question. If nostalgia helps, why does it sometimes hurt?
Because it's bittersweet by design. It reminds you something good happened, and that it's gone.
The comfort show lets you feel the sweet part, without staying too long in the bitter part.

So does rewatching mean you're stuck in the past?
Look at how fast the story moved.
*[timeline slides by, one icon per stop]*
In 1688, it was a disease.
In 1951, it became a rerun.
In 1968, it became a lab result.
In 1996, it became a teaching tool.
In 2020, it became a half-billion-dollar asset.
And on a random Tuesday night, it became your couch, your blanket, and an episode you could recite.

## [10:30–12:00] PUNCHLINE & CTA

Here's the last piece.
In 2012, two researchers interviewed people who reread books, rewatch films and revisit places.
*[card: RECONSUMPTION · Russell & Levy, 2012]*
One reason came up again and again. People went back to see how *they* had changed.
The show stays exactly the same. So it works like a mirror.
The joke you didn't get at fifteen lands at twenty-five.
The boss you laughed at starts to look a little like you.
The character who annoyed you at nineteen turns out to be the most honest one.
You notice a sad moment you used to skip, because now you've lived it.
*[the TV turns into a mirror; the stick figure in it is older]*
So here's the twist. You have never watched the same show twice.
The episode didn't change. The person watching it did.

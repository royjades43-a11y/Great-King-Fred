# PesaBrain EP04 — "401(k), IRA or Roth? The Order That Actually Matters"

Voiceover script for ElevenLabs, in 4 parts. About 1,600 words. At your pace in EP03 (about 165 words a minute)
that's roughly 9:30, comfortably over the 8-minute mid-roll mark. Each part ends on a hook, so the breaks between parts are natural ad slots.

It picks up from EP03's last line ("which account to put all this in first") and ends by teasing EP05 on debt.

## Part 1 — The hook: the box problem
```
Here's a strange way to lose money. Not by spending it. Not by investing it badly. Just by putting it in the wrong box.

Four-oh-one-k. I.R.A. Roth I.R.A. Roth four-oh-one-k. They sound like passwords a robot made up.

And when your brain sees a confusing choice... it does the one thing that feels safest. Nothing.

Psychologists call this choice overload. Researchers looking at real retirement plans found that for every ten extra funds a plan offered, fewer people signed up at all. More choices... less saving.

And doing nothing is not free. Remember the snowball from the last video? Every year it sits at the top of the hill is a year it never gets back.

So today, we're going to make this simple. In the last video, we built the snowball. Today, we pick where it lives.

Because the box you choose doesn't change how much your money grows. It changes how much of it you get to keep... and how much goes to taxes.

By the end of this video, you'll have a simple order. Step one, step two, step three. It works for most people. And step one is literally free money... that millions of Americans leave on the table every year.

But first, let's open the boxes. They're much less scary than they sound.

Every one of these accounts is just a container. Inside it, you put the same boring stuff from last time. Index funds. Target-date funds. The box doesn't change what you invest in. It only changes how it's taxed.

And there are really only two tax deals.

Deal one. Pay the tax later. You put money in before tax. It grows. And you pay income tax when you take it out in retirement. That's called a traditional account.

Deal two. Pay the tax now. You put money in after tax. It grows. And when you take it out in retirement... you pay nothing. Not on what you put in. Not on the growth. That's a Roth.

That's the whole secret. Traditional means tax later. Roth means tax now... and never again.

So which one wins? This is where your brain starts playing tricks again.
```

## Part 2 — Traditional vs. Roth
```
Think of it like a farmer.

A farmer can pay tax on the seeds... or pay tax on the harvest.

The seeds are small. The harvest is huge.

A traditional account taxes the harvest. A Roth taxes the seeds.

Let's put numbers on it. Say you put five hundred dollars a month into a Roth, from age twenty-five to sixty-five. It grows at seven percent a year, on average.

You put in about two hundred and forty thousand dollars. By sixty-five, the snowball has grown to roughly one point three million.

That's more than a million dollars of growth. And in a Roth... you never pay tax on any of it.

So why would anyone choose traditional?

Because your brain loves a reward today. A traditional account cuts your tax bill this year. You see it right away. The Roth's reward shows up decades later... and your brain is terrible at caring about future you.

Psychologists call it present bias. A dollar today feels much bigger than a dollar in thirty years. Even when the dollar in thirty years is worth far more.

But here's the honest part. Traditional isn't a trick. Sometimes it's the better deal.

The real question is simple. Will your tax rate be higher now... or later?

If you're early in your career, and your income is lower than it will probably be later, Roth usually makes a lot of sense. You pay tax while your rate is low, and your snowball grows tax-free forever.

If you're in your peak earning years, and your tax rate is high right now, traditional can make more sense. You skip tax at today's high rate, and pay later, hopefully at a lower one.

And if you honestly don't know? Many people split it. Some Roth. Some traditional. That way, whatever taxes look like in the future... you're covered either way.

There's one more thing that makes the Roth I.R.A. special. The money you put in... not the growth, just what you put in... can usually be taken back out at any time, without tax or penalty. That flexibility helps a lot of people feel safe enough to start.

But hold on. Before you pick Roth or traditional... there's a step that beats both of them.

And it's the step your brain is most likely to skip.
```

## Part 3 — The order: steps 1 to 3
```
First, the difference between a four-oh-one-k and an I.R.A.

A four-oh-one-k is the box your job gives you. Money comes straight out of your paycheck. Automatic. Exactly like we talked about last time.

An I.R.A. is a box you open yourself, at a brokerage. It stands for individual retirement account. Individual. Nobody opens it for you.

Both can be traditional or Roth. And now, here's the order.

Step one. Get the full match.

Many employers will match what you put into your four-oh-one-k, up to a limit. Say you earn sixty thousand dollars a year, and your employer matches dollar for dollar, up to five percent of your pay.

You put in three thousand dollars. They put in three thousand dollars. That's an instant one hundred percent return, before your money even starts growing.

No index fund. No stock. No crypto coin. Nothing else on Earth reliably does that.

If you take only one thing from this video, take this. Never leave the match on the table. Check your benefits page. Find the number. Put in at least enough to get all of it.

Step two. Protect the snowball.

Build your cash buffer, so an emergency never forces you to raid your retirement. And pay off high-interest debt, like credit cards. A twenty percent interest rate will eat your snowball faster than it can grow.

Step three. Open a Roth I.R.A.

After the match, many people put their next dollars into a Roth I.R.A. You choose where it lives. You choose the funds. And often, the fees are lower than in a work plan.

But here's a trap almost nobody warns you about.

When you put money into an I.R.A., it often lands as cash. And it sits there. As cash. Until you choose an investment.

Research from Vanguard found that a large share of people who moved money into an I.R.A. still had it sitting in cash years later. Not invested. Not growing. Just... waiting.

The box was right. But the snowball never left the top of the hill.

So when you open your I.R.A., do the last step. Actually buy the fund. A broad index fund, or a target-date fund. Then set up automatic contributions, so it keeps happening without you.

Three steps down. But there's still one more box. And one mistake that can quietly cost you thousands... usually right when you change jobs.
```

## Part 4 — Steps 4 and 5, the job-change mistake, and the close
```
Step four. Go back to your four-oh-one-k.

Once you've filled your I.R.A. for the year, put more into your four-oh-one-k. Every year, the government sets a limit for how much you can put in. Try to raise your contribution a little each year, especially every time you get a raise, until you get close to that limit.

Step five. If you've done all of that... and you still have money to invest... congratulations. Open a regular brokerage account. No special tax deal. But the same boring snowball. Same index funds. Same patience.

That's the whole order. Match. Protect. Roth I.R.A. Back to the four-oh-one-k. Then everything else.

Now, the mistake.

You change jobs. You get a letter about your old four-oh-one-k. And your brain sees a big number and thinks... I could just cash this out.

Please don't.

If you cash out a retirement account before age fifty-nine and a half, you'll usually owe income tax on it... plus a ten percent penalty. A big chunk of your snowball can disappear in one afternoon.

And worse, you lose all the growth that money would have had. Decades of it.

Instead, roll it over. Move it into your new employer's plan, or into an I.R.A. And then... remember the trap... make sure it's actually invested.

So let's put it all together.

The boxes aren't scary. Traditional means tax later. Roth means tax now, and never again.

And the order is simple. Get the full match. Protect the snowball with a cash buffer and no high-interest debt. Open a Roth I.R.A. and actually invest it. Go back and raise your four-oh-one-k. Then everything else.

Your brain will want to wait until you understand every rule. Until you find the perfect account. The perfect fund. The perfect moment.

But a good box you start today... beats a perfect box you start in five years.

Pick the box. Put the snowball in. Push.

And if there's something stopping you from even getting to step one... like a credit card balance that never seems to shrink, no matter how much you pay... that's not just bad luck. It's a trap your brain walks right into.

And in the next video, we're going to break out of it.
```

## Fact notes (check before recording)
- **Choice overload:** Sethi-Iyengar, Huberman & Jiang (2004), "How Much Choice Is Too Much? Contributions to 401(k) Retirement Plans."
  Participation fell about 1.5 to 2 percentage points for every 10 extra funds offered.
- **Roth example:** $500/month from 25 to 65 at 7%, compounded monthly ≈ $1.31M on $240,000 contributed. Illustrative only.
  $500/month is under the Roth IRA annual limit.
- **Contribution limits change every year, so the script avoids saying them.** For 2026, the IRS set the 401(k) employee limit
  at $24,500 and the IRA limit at $7,500 (higher catch-up amounts at 50+). Confirm on irs.gov before you put numbers in the description.
- **Roth IRA income limits:** above certain incomes you can't contribute directly. Worth a line in the description.
- **Roth IRA contributions** (not earnings) can generally be withdrawn at any time without tax or penalty. Earnings have separate rules (age 59½ and the 5-year rule).
- **Early withdrawal:** before 59½, retirement withdrawals are usually taxed as income plus a 10% additional tax, with some exceptions.
- **Uninvested IRA cash:** Vanguard research (2023, "The cost of cash in IRAs") found that a large share of rollover IRA
  investors still held their money in cash years after rolling over. The script deliberately says "a large share" rather than an exact percentage.
- **The order** (match → buffer and high-interest debt → Roth IRA → more 401(k) → taxable) is a common rule of thumb, not personal advice.
  Keep the "not financial advice" line in the description.

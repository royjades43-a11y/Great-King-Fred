## Description

```
You're lost. There's a person right there. Instead, you walk around the same block three times. Psychologists have tested this with thousands of strangers, and the people who won't ask are wrong about almost everything: how many people will say yes, how they'll feel, and how they'll see you.

In this video: how Benjamin Franklin won over a rival by borrowing a book, why strangers say yes about twice as often as we expect (even to writing "pickle" in a library book), the three fears that stop us asking, what never asking really costs, and the one thing that makes people say yes.

📚 Research and sources mentioned
• Benjamin Franklin, Autobiography: the rival and the borrowed book ("the Ben Franklin effect")
• Jecker & Landy (1969), Human Relations: liking a person more after doing them a favor
• Flynn & Lake (2008), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology: people overestimate how many they need to ask
• Bohns (2016), Current Directions in Psychological Science: across 14,000+ strangers, yes-rates underestimated by about half
• Bohns, Roghanizad & Xu (2014), Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin: the "pickle" study
• Zhao & Epley (2022), Psychological Science: helpers feel better, and less inconvenienced, than askers expect
• Brooks, Gino & Schweitzer (2015), Management Science: asking for advice makes you look more competent
• Roghanizad & Bohns (2017), Journal of Experimental Social Psychology: face-to-face requests about 34 times more effective than email
• Roghanizad & Bohns (2022), Social Psychological and Personality Science: video and phone requests
```

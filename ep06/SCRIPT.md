# EP06 — How Much Money Should You Keep in the Bank? (Most People Get This Wrong)

Voiceover script for ElevenLabs, in 4 parts. About 1,550 words, roughly 9:15 at your usual pace.
It picks up EP05's last line ("How much is actually enough to keep in the bank?") and each part ends on a hook,
so the breaks between parts are natural mid-roll slots.

---

## PART 1

Your car makes a noise. Not a big noise. Just a small, new noise that wasn't there yesterday.

And your stomach drops. Because you already know how this ends. A mechanic, a clipboard, and a number. Twelve hundred dollars.

Here's the real question. Where does that twelve hundred dollars come from?

For a lot of people, it doesn't come from anywhere. In the Federal Reserve's latest survey, only 63% of American adults said they could cover a surprise $400 expense with cash or its equivalent. And in a 2026 survey by Bankrate, just 30% said they would pay a $1,000 emergency from their savings.

So what does everybody else do? They swipe. And if you watched our last video, you know exactly where that leads. A $1,200 repair at 22% interest, paid off with the minimum, becomes a trap that follows you for years.

An emergency fund is the wall between you and that trap. By the end of this video, you'll know exactly how much yours should be, where to keep it so your brain can't touch it, and a simple way to build it without feeling poorer.

But first, let's talk about why almost nobody has one. Because it's not that people are lazy. Your brain is working against you.

The first reason is something psychologists call optimism bias. When we imagine the future, we picture the good version. Your car keeps running. Your job is safe. Your teeth are fine. Bad things happen, sure. But they happen to other people.

Here's the problem. Emergencies aren't rare. Over a lifetime, they're almost guaranteed. The car will break. Someone will get sick. A job will end. The only thing you don't know is the date.

An emergency isn't a question of if. It's a surprise about when.

And there's a second reason your brain resists saving for it. A sneakier one. Because to your brain, an emergency fund looks like money doing absolutely nothing.

## PART 2

Think about it. If you've watched our boring way to get rich video, you know money invested can grow into a snowball. So money just sitting in the bank feels like a waste. Lazy money. Money that could be working.

So your brain says, why keep thousands of dollars sitting there, earning almost nothing?

Here's the twist. Your emergency fund isn't an investment. It's insurance. And its return isn't the interest it earns. Its return is everything it stops from happening.

It stops a $1,200 repair from becoming a credit card balance at 22%. It stops you from raiding your 401(k) and paying taxes and penalties on it, like we saw two videos ago. And it stops something that doesn't show up on any statement at all.

Your brain.

In 2013, researchers published a study in the journal Science. They found that when people with money problems were made to think about those problems, they did worse on tests of thinking and self-control. Princeton described the size of the effect as similar to losing 13 IQ points, or a full night's sleep.

Money worry takes up space in your head. It follows you to work, to dinner, to bed. And a brain that's busy worrying makes worse decisions, including worse decisions about money.

Now look at the other side. In 2025, Vanguard surveyed more than 12,000 investors. People with at least $2,000 in emergency savings reported 21% higher financial well-being than people with no emergency savings at all. Even after accounting for income and wealth.

And people with no emergency savings said they spent about 7 hours a week dealing with their finances. People with a cushion? Less than 4.

So that money isn't doing nothing. It's buying you something that money usually can't. Peace of mind, and a brain that works properly.

Okay. So you need one. The real question is, how much? And it's probably not the number you've heard.

## PART 3

You've probably heard the classic rule. Keep three to six months of expenses.

It's a good rule. But most people get one word wrong. Expenses, not income. And not all your expenses. Just the ones that keep your life running.

Rent or mortgage. Food. Utilities. Insurance. Transport. Minimum debt payments. That's it. Not streaming, not eating out, not that gym you don't go to. If you lost your income tomorrow, those would be the first things you'd cut.

Let's do it for Bean. Bean's essentials come to $2,800 a month. Three months is $8,400. Six months is $16,800.

And this is where most people quit. Because $16,800 is a terrifying number. And your brain hates goals that feel impossible. Psychologists call this the goal-gradient effect. We try harder the closer we get to a goal, and we barely try at all when the finish line feels miles away.

So don't build one big fund. Build it in three levels.

Level one: $1,000. This is your starter fund, and it handles most of life's small disasters. The car repair. The vet bill. The flight home you didn't plan for.

Level two: one month of essentials. For Bean, $2,800. Now a surprise doesn't just get covered. It doesn't even hurt.

Level three: three to six months. This is the one that protects you from the big one. A layoff, an illness, a move you didn't choose.

So, three or six? Lean toward three months if you have a steady job, and maybe a second income in the house. Lean toward six, or even more, if you're self-employed, paid on commission, the only earner in the home, or you have kids depending on you.

And yes, there is such a thing as too much. Once you're past about six months, extra cash sitting in the bank slowly loses value to inflation. That's when the extra should start rolling into your own snowball.

But how much you keep is only half the answer. Where you keep it matters just as much. Because the moment you build it, your brain will start looking for reasons to spend it.

## PART 4

Most people keep their emergency money in their checking account. Big mistake.

Money you can see is money you'll spend. If your emergency fund sits next to your spending money, it slowly turns into a vacation, a new phone, a weekend out. Not all at once. Just a little at a time.

So move it out of sight.

Put it in a separate high-yield savings account, ideally at a different bank from your everyday account. Three things happen. You don't see it every time you open your banking app. Moving it takes a day or two, which is just enough friction to stop impulse spending. And it earns interest that's often several times what a typical savings account pays.

Then give it a name. Not "Savings." Call it "Emergency Only." Or "Car Breaks." Or "Do Not Touch."

That sounds silly, but it works. Psychologists call it earmarking. Your brain sorts money into mental buckets, and money with a job is harder to spend. In one study in India, families who were given labelled envelopes for their savings saved more. And when the envelopes had a picture of their children on them, they saved even more.

Now, build it without feeling it. Set up an automatic transfer on payday, before the money ever reaches your spending account. Even $50 a paycheck. And when extra money shows up, like a tax refund or a bonus, send part of it straight to the fund.

One more rule. Decide what counts as an emergency before it happens. It has to be unexpected, necessary, and urgent. Christmas is not an emergency. It comes every year. A vacation is not an emergency. And when you do use the fund, refill it next.

Let's go back to Bean. Two years later, the car makes the noise again. Same mechanic. Same clipboard. Twelve hundred dollars.

But this time, Bean's stomach doesn't drop. Bean moves the money from the account called "Car Breaks." No card. No interest. No trap. And four months later, the fund is full again.

Remember what we learned in why you'll never feel rich? It's not your salary that makes you feel secure. It's the money in the bank. This is what that feels like. Not luxury. Calm.

So here's your plan. Work out your monthly essentials. Start with $1,000. Then one month. Then three to six. Keep it out of sight, give it a name, and automate it.

Because the emergency is coming. You just get to decide whether it's a disaster, or just a Tuesday.

And speaking of bad days. Why do so many of us fix a bad mood by buying something? And why does that feeling disappear before the package even arrives? That's the next video.

## Fact notes (for the description; check before publishing)
- **Federal Reserve, Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households in 2025 (SHED, May 2026):** 63% of adults would cover a hypothetical $400 emergency
  expense using only cash or its equivalent (cash, savings, or a card paid off at the next statement). Unchanged from 2024.
- **Bankrate, 2026 Annual Emergency Savings Report (Jan 2026):** 30% would pay a $1,000 emergency expense from savings.
- **Mani, Mullainathan, Shafir & Zhao (2013), "Poverty Impedes Cognitive Function," Science.** The "13 IQ points / a night's sleep"
  comparison is Princeton's description of the effect size (press release), so the script attributes it to Princeton.
- **Vanguard (2025) research, about 12,400 investors, CFPB Financial Well-Being scale:** at least $2,000 in emergency savings → 21% higher
  financial well-being than no emergency savings, after controlling for income, education and assets. Hours on finances: 7.3 vs 3.7 a week.
  Correlational, not causal; the script says "reported".
- **Goal-gradient effect:** Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng (2006), Journal of Marketing Research.
- **Earmarking:** Soman & Cheema (2011), "Earmarking and Partitioning: Increasing Saving by Low-Income Households," Journal of Marketing
  Research (field study with labourers in rural India; envelopes, and envelopes with children's photos).
- **Optimism bias:** Weinstein (1980); Sharot (2011).
- **High-yield savings:** in November 2026 top accounts pay around 4% and the national average is well under 1%, so the script says
  "often several times" rather than giving a rate (rates change).
- **3–6 months of essential expenses** is a common rule of thumb, not personal advice. Add the not-financial-advice line to the description.

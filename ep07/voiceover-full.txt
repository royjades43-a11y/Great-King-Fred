Here's a question. Two groups of people are offered the exact same thing. One group just watched a sad movie scene. The other watched a nature documentary. Which group pays more?

Most people guess the happy ones. Good mood, open wallet.

It's the sad ones.

In a study published in 2004, a short, sad film clip made people willing to pay more for the very same item. And in a follow-up, sad people who were focused on themselves paid even more.

Nothing about the product changed. Only their mood.

Psychologists call this a carryover effect. A feeling from one part of your life spills into a decision that has nothing to do with it. A fight at work. A rainy commute. A text you didn't want to read. And twenty minutes later... you're paying more for headphones.

And next week, that matters more than ever. Because next week is Black Friday. Last year, a record two hundred and three million Americans shopped over Thanksgiving weekend. And stores know exactly how to reach the version of you that's had a long, bad week.

So why does sadness make us spend?

Here's the surprising part. Retail therapy is real.

In 2014, researchers found that making shopping choices helped people feel less sad. Not because of the stuff. Because of control. Sadness often comes with the feeling that life is happening to you. Choosing what to buy... gives a little bit of control back.

And it only worked for sadness. When people were angry, shopping choices didn't help the same way. Different feeling, different fix.

So your brain isn't stupid. It found a painkiller that works.

But a painkiller isn't a cure. And this one comes with an expiry date. It runs out... before the box even reaches your door.

Picture Bean. It's been a rough Monday. He's on the sofa, scrolling. He finds the perfect jacket. He adds it to the cart.

And right there, his mood lifts. Not when he wears it. Not when it arrives. When he clicks "buy."

Researchers have seen this pattern. In a 2013 study, people who expected a purchase to change their life felt their happiest before they bought it... and less happy after.

The wanting felt better than the having.

Think about the last thing you were excited to buy. The searching. The comparing. The reviews. Picturing yourself with it. That was the fun part. Owning it... became normal in about a week. If you watched why you'll never feel rich, you know that feeling. It's the hedonic treadmill... at the speed of next-day delivery.

Thursday, the box arrives. Bean opens it. The jacket is nice. It's just a jacket. And the bad week? Still there. Now with a bill attached.

So what does Bean do?

He scrolls again.

That's the loop. Feel bad. Buy something. Feel a little better. The feeling fades. Feel bad... plus a bill. Buy something.

It's not a character flaw. It's a very human way to handle a feeling. But it's an expensive one. And it never actually fixes the thing that made you sad.

Now here's why next week is dangerous. Black Friday is built to catch you right at the top of that loop... at the exact moment the wanting feels best. And it uses three tricks to do it.

Trick number one. The countdown.

"Deal ends in ten minutes." "Only two left." A timer that's always ticking.

Urgency makes your brain skip the question "do I need this?" and jump to "don't miss out." And sometimes the clock isn't even real. In 2022, the Federal Trade Commission published a report on "dark patterns," and it called out fake countdown timers that simply reset when they hit zero... and "low stock" messages when there's plenty.

How to beat it? Ask one question: if this timer didn't exist... would I still want it?

Trick number two. The "was" price.

That big crossed-out number. Was two hundred dollars. Now ninety-nine. Remember anchoring from our credit card video? The first number you see becomes your starting point. Ninety-nine feels like a steal next to two hundred... even if almost nobody ever paid two hundred. U.S. rules actually say a "was" price is supposed to be a real price the item genuinely sold for. The fact that regulators needed that rule tells you everything.

How to beat it? Ignore the crossed-out number completely. The only question is: is ninety-nine dollars worth it... to me?

Trick number three. And this one's a puzzle. Try it.

A hoodie is thirty percent off. At checkout, you get an extra twenty percent off. How much did you save in total? Fifty percent?

Lock it in.

It's forty-four. The extra twenty percent comes off the price that's already been cut. A hundred dollars becomes seventy... then fifty-six.

Thirty percent off, plus another thirty? Not sixty. Fifty-one.

Stores split discounts on purpose. Two numbers feel bigger than one. The trick to beat it? Don't add what you save. Multiply what you keep. Seventy percent times eighty percent... you pay fifty-six.

Try it on any sale this week. You'll be surprised how often a big, exciting number shrinks the moment you do the math.

So, three tricks, one goal: get you to buy fast, with a feeling, before your thinking brain shows up. How do you enjoy Black Friday... without falling for it?

Four moves. Use them next week.

Move one. The mood check. Before you hit "buy," ask one question: am I buying the thing... or the feeling? Just naming the feeling takes some of its power away. If the answer is "the feeling," close the app and get that sense of control somewhere free. Remember, the relief came from control, not from the stuff. Tidy one room. Finish one small task. Go for a walk. Call someone. Ten minutes of anything you choose.

Move two. The twenty-four-hour cart. Put it in the cart, close the tab, decide tomorrow. A good deal will usually still be there. And if the timer magically resets... well, now you know.

Move three. Fun money, decided in advance. Pick a Black Friday budget today, while you're calm. Then spend it, guilt-free. Planned fun is still fun. It just doesn't come with regret.

Move four. Shop the list, not the deal. Write down what you actually need before the sales start. If it's on the list and it's on sale, that's a win. If it's not on the list, it's not a deal. It's a trick with a discount.

The bad week still happened. But this time, it didn't come with a bill.

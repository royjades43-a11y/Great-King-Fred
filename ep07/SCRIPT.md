# EP07 — Why You Spend More When You're Sad (Black Friday Knows It)

Voiceover script for ElevenLabs, in 4 parts. About 1,400 words, roughly 8½–9 minutes. It keeps EP06's ending promise ("Why do so many
of us fix a bad mood by buying something?") and every part ends on a hook, so the breaks between parts work as mid-roll slots.
**Publish by about Nov 25** to catch Black Friday searches.

This is the first episode in the **new presentation style** (see "How it's presented" under each part).

---

## PART 1

Here's a question. Two groups of people are offered the exact same thing. One group just watched a sad movie scene. The other watched a nature documentary. Which group pays more?

Most people guess the happy ones. Good mood, open wallet.

It's the sad ones.

In a study published in 2004, a short, sad film clip made people willing to pay more for the very same item. And in a follow-up, sad people who were focused on themselves paid even more.

Nothing about the product changed. Only their mood.

Psychologists call this a carryover effect. A feeling from one part of your life spills into a decision that has nothing to do with it. A fight at work. A rainy commute. A text you didn't want to read. And twenty minutes later... you're paying more for headphones.

And next week, that matters more than ever. Because next week is Black Friday. Last year, a record two hundred and three million Americans shopped over Thanksgiving weekend. And stores know exactly how to reach the version of you that's had a long, bad week.

By the end of this video, you'll know why a bad mood makes you spend, why the good feeling disappears before the package even arrives, the three Black Friday tricks built to catch you, and a simple plan to enjoy the sales... without the regret.

So why does sadness make us spend?

Here's the surprising part. Retail therapy is real.

In 2014, researchers found that making shopping choices helped people feel less sad. Not because of the stuff. Because of control. Sadness often comes with the feeling that life is happening to you. Choosing what to buy... gives a little bit of control back.

And it only worked for sadness. When people were angry, shopping choices didn't help the same way. Different feeling, different fix.

So your brain isn't stupid. It found a painkiller that works.

But a painkiller isn't a cure. And this one comes with an expiry date. It runs out... before the box even reaches your door.

### How it's presented (Part 1)
- **World:** Bean's apartment on a rainy evening. One continuous set: the sofa, the window with rain, a phone glowing in Bean's hands, the door in the background. The camera starts wide and pushes in during the question.
- **Hook:** two rows of little people icons appear on two sofas, one under a raincloud and one under a sun. A price tag hangs between them. As "Which group pays more?" lands, the tag swings to the **sun** side (the tempting answer)... then on "It's the sad ones," it **swings back** to the rain side and grows bigger. That's the wow moment.
- **Bean presents:** Bean sits on the sofa, turns to camera on "Nothing about the product changed," and his mouth moves with the voice.
- **Black Friday:** the window outside turns into a city street with a giant **BLACK FRIDAY** billboard flickering on. **203,000,000** counts up across the billboard.
- **The promise:** a checklist of 4 items slides onto the wall (it stays in the corner for the whole video and ticks off as we go).
- **Control:** Bean holds the phone, taps a choice, and a small lever labelled **CONTROL** clicks from OFF to ON. The raincloud above him shrinks a little.
- **Ending:** a little medicine bottle labelled **RETAIL THERAPY** with an expiry date stamped on it. The camera pushes in on the date.

## PART 2

Picture Bean. It's been a rough Monday. He's on the sofa, scrolling. He finds the perfect jacket. He adds it to the cart.

And right there, his mood lifts. Not when he wears it. Not when it arrives. When he clicks "buy."

Researchers have seen this pattern. In a 2013 study, people who expected a purchase to change their life felt their happiest before they bought it... and less happy after.

The wanting felt better than the having.

Think about the last thing you were excited to buy. The searching. The comparing. The reviews. Picturing yourself with it. That was the fun part. Owning it... became normal in about a week. If you watched why you'll never feel rich, you know that feeling. It's the hedonic treadmill... at the speed of next-day delivery.

Thursday, the box arrives. Bean opens it. The jacket is nice. It's just a jacket. And the bad week? Still there. Now with a bill attached.

So what does Bean do?

He scrolls again.

That's the loop. Feel bad. Buy something. Feel a little better. The feeling fades. Feel bad... plus a bill. Buy something.

It's not a character flaw. It's a very human way to handle a feeling. But it's an expensive one. And it never actually fixes the thing that made you sad.

Now here's why next week is dangerous. Black Friday is built to catch you right at the top of that loop... at the exact moment the wanting feels best. And it uses three tricks to do it.

### How it's presented (Part 2)
- **World:** the same apartment, and the camera stays. Bean on the sofa, rain outside.
- **The anticipation curve:** a mood line draws across the wall like a heartbeat monitor, live with the voice. It rises as Bean scrolls, **peaks at the "BUY" button**, and drops after. When the box arrives Thursday, the line is back at the bottom. The camera follows the line's peak.
- **The box arrives:** a doorbell, the door opens, a box slides in. Bean opens it: a jacket. A tiny "meh" bubble.
- **The bill:** an envelope slides under the door and lands next to the box.
- **The loop:** the camera pulls up to a top-down view. Bean's evening becomes a circle of 5 icons (raincloud → phone → sparkle → fading sparkle → raincloud + bill) with a small Bean running around it.
- **Ending:** the circle's top point (the BUY glow) flashes. A shop sign drops onto it: **BLACK FRIDAY: OPEN HERE**.

## PART 3

Trick number one. The countdown.

"Deal ends in ten minutes." "Only two left." A timer that's always ticking.

Urgency makes your brain skip the question "do I need this?" and jump to "don't miss out." And sometimes the clock isn't even real. In 2022, the Federal Trade Commission published a report on "dark patterns," and it called out fake countdown timers that simply reset when they hit zero... and "low stock" messages when there's plenty.

How to beat it? Ask one question: if this timer didn't exist... would I still want it?

Trick number two. The "was" price.

That big crossed-out number. Was two hundred dollars. Now ninety-nine. Remember anchoring from our credit card video? The first number you see becomes your starting point. Ninety-nine feels like a steal next to two hundred... even if almost nobody ever paid two hundred. U.S. rules actually say a "was" price is supposed to be a real price the item genuinely sold for. The fact that regulators needed that rule tells you everything.

How to beat it? Ignore the crossed-out number completely. The only question is: is ninety-nine dollars worth it... to me?

Trick number three. And this one's a puzzle. Try it.

A hoodie is thirty percent off. At checkout, you get an extra twenty percent off. How much did you save in total? Fifty percent?

Lock it in.

It's forty-four. The extra twenty percent comes off the price that's already been cut. A hundred dollars becomes seventy... then fifty-six.

Thirty percent off, plus another thirty? Not sixty. Fifty-one.

Stores split discounts on purpose. Two numbers feel bigger than one. The trick to beat it? Don't add what you save. Multiply what you keep. Seventy percent times eighty percent... you pay fifty-six.

Try it on any sale this week. You'll be surprised how often a big, exciting number shrinks the moment you do the math.

So, three tricks, one goal: get you to buy fast, with a feeling, before your thinking brain shows up. How do you enjoy Black Friday... without falling for it?

### How it's presented (Part 3)
- **World:** Bean steps out of his door and the camera follows him into one long **Black Friday mall** (a wide set we pan along). Impulse is the "salesperson" behind each counter. Each trick is a shop window we pan to.
- **Trick 1 (Countdown Shop):** a big digital timer above the door reads 00:10:00. On "simply reset," it hits 00:00 and **flips back to 10:00**. Impulse winks. An "ONLY 2 LEFT!" sign, then the camera peeks behind the counter at a mountain of identical boxes. The camera shakes slightly on the reset.
- **Trick 2 (The "Was" Shop):** a huge price tag: **WAS $200** crossed out, **NOW $99**. The EP05 anchor drops on a chain and hooks onto "$200". Prof. Calc walks in holding a sign: **"WAS" = A REAL PAST PRICE**.
- **Trick 3 (Stacked-Discount Shop):** one hoodie on a hanger with a tag: **$100**.
  - "30% off": the tag animates to **$70**.
  - "extra 20%": Impulse shouts **"50% OFF!!"** in a bubble.
  - **"Lock it in."** A 3-2-1 countdown, and a sticky note "YOUR ANSWER?" points at the comment button.
  - The reveal: the tag goes $70 → **$56**. A big **44%** slams in and the "50%" bubble pops.
  - Then a second hoodie: $100 → $70 → $49, giving **51%, not 60%**.
  - Prof. Calc writes **0.7 × 0.8 = 0.56** on a chalk sign. The camera does a close-up on the numbers.
- **The corner checklist** ticks item 3 ("the 3 tricks").
- **Ending:** the mall lights flicker, and Bean is seen at the exit holding three shopping bags, frozen mid-step.

## PART 4

Four moves. Use them next week.

Move one. The mood check. Before you hit "buy," ask one question: am I buying the thing... or the feeling? Just naming the feeling takes some of its power away. If the answer is "the feeling," close the app and get that sense of control somewhere free. Remember, the relief came from control, not from the stuff. Tidy one room. Finish one small task. Go for a walk. Call someone. Ten minutes of anything you choose.

Move two. The twenty-four-hour cart. Put it in the cart, close the tab, decide tomorrow. A good deal will usually still be there. And if the timer magically resets... well, now you know.

Move three. Fun money, decided in advance. Pick a Black Friday budget today, while you're calm. Then spend it, guilt-free. Planned fun is still fun. It just doesn't come with regret.

Move four. Shop the list, not the deal. Write down what you actually need before the sales start. If it's on the list and it's on sale, that's a win. If it's not on the list, it's not a deal. It's a trick with a discount.

Back to Bean. Same rough week. Black Friday arrives. The timer's ticking. Ninety-nine dollars, was two hundred.

But this time, Bean checks in. "Am I buying the jacket... or the feeling?" He puts it in the cart, and calls a friend instead. The next day, he buys the two things on his list, with the fun money he set aside. Nothing else.

The bad week still happened. But this time, it didn't come with a bill.

And here's the part nobody says about Black Friday. The best deal is often the one you didn't make. Every dollar Bean didn't spend went exactly where we put it in the last video... into the account called "Car Breaks."

So here's the truth. Shopping isn't the enemy. Shopping to fix a feeling is. Your mood changes what you'll pay, the joy peaks before the box arrives, and Black Friday is designed to catch you at exactly that moment.

Check the feeling. Wait a day. Spend the fun money you planned. Shop the list.

And speaking of other people... why do your friends decide how much you spend? That's the next video.

### How it's presented (Part 4)
- **World:** Bean walks back home through the mall. Each move is a doorway he passes through on the way out (one continuous pan). The checklist in the corner fills up.
- **Move 1:** Bean stops at a mirror. The bubble over his head splits into two: **THE THING** (a jacket) / **THE FEELING** (a little raincloud). He taps the raincloud and it shrinks.
- **Move 2:** a shopping cart rolls into a little "fridge" labelled **24H**. A clock spins forward, the fridge opens, and the cart comes out with fewer items.
- **Move 3:** a jar labelled **FUN MONEY** fills. Bean high-fives it.
- **Move 4:** a handwritten list; items on the list get a gold **WIN** tag, and an off-list item's "deal" tag turns red: **TRICK**.
- **Bean's story:** we're back in the same apartment as Part 1, shot for shot (same angle, same rain), but this time Bean puts the phone down and calls a friend. The rain outside softens. Then: a box on the doorstep with **2 items** and a **FUN MONEY** receipt. No bill under the door.
- **Close:** the corner checklist is all ticked, and the four moves line up as a final card.
- **End card:** "WHY YOUR FRIENDS DECIDE WHAT YOU SPEND" with a play button. Bean and two friends at a café, with a big bill on the table.

## Fact notes (citations for the description)
- **Lerner, J. S., Small, D. A., & Loewenstein, G. (2004).** "Heart Strings and Purse Strings: Carryover Effects of Emotions on
  Economic Decisions." *Psychological Science*, 15(5), 337–341. A sad film clip (from *The Champ*) raised buying prices and lowered
  selling prices for the same item. The script doesn't quote a percentage, because the sizes reported in press coverage vary.
- **Cryder, C. E., Lerner, J. S., Gross, J. J., & Dahl, R. E. (2008).** "Misery Is Not Miserly: Sad and Self-Focused Individuals
  Spend More." *Psychological Science*, 19(6), 525–530. Sad participants who were more self-focused paid more (sad clip vs. a
  National Geographic coral reef clip).
- **Rick, S. I., Pereira, B., & Burson, K. A. (2014).** "The Benefits of Retail Therapy: Making Purchase Decisions Reduces Residual
  Sadness." *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 24(3), 373–380. Shopping choices (hypothetical or real) reduced lingering sadness by
  restoring a sense of personal control; the benefit didn't extend to anger.
- **Richins, M. L. (2013).** "When Wanting Is Better Than Having." *Journal of Consumer Research*, 40(1), 1–18. Highly materialistic
  consumers showed positive emotions rising before purchase and declining after. The script says "people who expected a purchase
  to change their life", which matches the paper's "transformation expectations".
- **National Retail Federation (Dec 2, 2025):** a record 202.9 million shoppers over Thanksgiving weekend 2025 (from an NRF/Prosper
  Insights survey). The script rounds this to "two hundred and three million".
- **FTC Staff Report (Sept 2022), "Bringing Dark Patterns to Light":** flags fake countdown timers that reset and false low-stock or
  high-demand messages. I checked this through law-firm summaries; open the FTC PDF before quoting it word for word.
- **FTC Guides Against Deceptive Pricing, 16 CFR Part 233.1:** a former-price comparison should use a price at which the item was
  actually offered, openly and in good faith, for a reasonable time.
- **The maths:** $100 × 0.7 = $70; × 0.8 = $56 (44% off). $100 × 0.7 × 0.7 = $49 (51% off).
- The four moves are general behavioural tips, not personal financial advice. Add the not-advice line to the description.

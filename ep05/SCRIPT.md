# PesaBrain EP05 — "Why Your Credit Card Balance Never Shrinks (And How to Break the Trap)"

Voiceover script for ElevenLabs, in 4 parts. About 1,550 words, roughly 9:30 at your usual pace. Each part ends on a hook,
so the breaks between parts are natural mid-roll ad slots.
It picks up EP04's last line ("...a credit card balance that never seems to shrink...").

## Part 1 — The hook: the number that tricks you
```
Every month, you pay your credit card bill. On time. Every single month.

And every month... the balance barely moves.

It feels like bad luck. Like the card is broken. Like you're just bad with money.

You're not. You're inside a trap. And it was built around one tiny number on your statement.

By the end of this video, you'll know exactly how that trap works... the two ways out... and the one phone call that can make the whole thing shrink faster. Most people never make it.

Let's start with the number.

Open any credit card statement and you'll find it. Minimum payment due.

Say you owe six thousand dollars, at twenty-two percent interest. Your minimum payment might be around a hundred and seventy dollars.

It looks reasonable. Affordable, even. So you pay it. Responsible, right?

Here's what that minimum hides.

If you only ever pay the minimum on that card... and the minimum keeps shrinking as your balance shrinks... it can take you more than twenty years to pay it off.

And you'll pay almost sixteen thousand dollars. For six thousand dollars of stuff.

Ten thousand dollars... just for the privilege of paying slowly.

The law actually makes card companies print a warning about this on your statement. A little box that shows how long the minimum will take. Most people never read it.

Because your brain isn't looking at that box. It's looking at the big, friendly number at the top.

And that's the first trick.
```

## Part 2 — The tricks: anchoring and the interest treadmill
```
Psychologists call it anchoring.

When your brain sees a number first, it uses that number as a starting point... even when it shouldn't.

In a study published in two thousand and nine, researcher Neil Stewart showed people credit card bills. Some bills showed a minimum payment. Some didn't.

When the minimum payment was printed on the bill, people chose to repay less. A lot less. Just seeing that small number pulled their payment down toward it.

The minimum isn't a suggestion. It's an anchor. And it's dragging your payment to the bottom.

Now here's the second trick. The treadmill.

Remember the snowball from our boring way to get rich video? Compound interest working for you, growing bigger every year.

Credit card debt is the same snowball... rolling the wrong way. Toward you.

Every month, the card adds interest to your balance. Twenty-two percent a year. Then next month, it charges interest on that interest.

So when you pay a hundred and seventy dollars, a big chunk of it never touches your balance. On that first payment, more than a hundred dollars goes straight to interest.

You're running. You're sweating. You're paying every month. And you're standing in the same place.

That's why the balance never seems to shrink. It's not bad luck. It's math, plus a brain that trusts the wrong number.

And there's a third trick that keeps people stuck. Your brain hates looking at debt. It feels like a failure. So it does the comfortable thing. It looks away.

Psychologists call it the ostrich effect. Studies have found that people check their accounts more when things look good... and less when things look bad. Exactly when looking matters most.

So how do you break out? There are two famous methods. And they disagree.
```

## Part 3 — Two ways out: avalanche vs snowball
```
First, the rule for both. Pick one fixed monthly amount, bigger than the minimum, and pay it every month. No matter what the statement suggests.

Remember that six thousand dollar card? If you just kept paying that first hundred and seventy dollars, every month, instead of following the shrinking minimum... you'd be done in under five years, not twenty. And you'd save around six thousand dollars in interest.

Same payment. One decision. Six thousand dollars.

Now, if you have more than one debt, you need an order.

Method one. The avalanche. List your debts from the highest interest rate to the lowest. Pay the minimum on everything... and throw every extra dollar at the highest rate first.

Mathematically, this is the winner. It always costs the least.

Method two. The snowball. List your debts from the smallest balance to the largest. Pay the minimum on everything... and throw every extra dollar at the smallest one first.

Let's compare. Three cards. Six thousand dollars total. Three hundred dollars a month.

With the avalanche, you're debt-free in about twenty-five months. With the snowball... about twenty-six months, and around three hundred dollars more in interest.

So the avalanche wins, right?

Here's the twist. With the snowball, your first card is gone in month five. With the avalanche, you wait until month fifteen.

And that matters more than you'd think.

Researchers at Northwestern University, Kellogg School of Management, studied real people paying off debt. They found that people who paid off whole accounts, closing out small debts completely, were more likely to eventually eliminate all their debt.

Small wins keep you going. Your brain needs proof that it's working.

So which one should you pick? If you're motivated by numbers, go avalanche. If you've tried before and quit... go snowball. The best method is the one you'll actually finish.

But both methods have the same enemy. That twenty-two percent interest rate. And there's a way to attack it directly.
```

## Part 4 — Shrink the rate, stop the bleeding, and the close
```
Move one. Call your card company and ask for a lower interest rate.

That's it. One phone call. Tell them you've been a customer for a while, and you'd like a lower rate. Surveys of cardholders have found that a large share of people who ask... get one.

Your brain will tell you it's awkward. Do it anyway. It might be the most valuable ten minutes of your year.

Move two. Look at a balance transfer. Some cards offer zero percent interest for a year or more on balances you move over. There's usually a fee, often three to five percent, and the rate jumps when the offer ends. But used carefully, it can stop the interest snowball cold... so every payment hits the actual balance.

Move three. Stop adding to the pile. Take the card out of your phone wallet. Out of your saved payments. Remember the pain of paying? Put some of it back.

And if the debt feels too big to handle alone, nonprofit credit counseling agencies can help you build a plan, sometimes with lower rates. Look for one that's accredited, and be careful with anyone who promises to make your debt disappear.

So here's the truth.

Your credit card balance doesn't shrink... because the system is designed around a small, friendly number that your brain trusts.

The minimum payment is an anchor. The interest is a snowball rolling the wrong way. And looking away only makes it bigger.

So pick a fixed payment, bigger than the minimum. Pick a method, avalanche or snowball. Make the phone call. And stop feeding the pile.

Every dollar you free from interest... is a dollar you can finally put into your own snowball. The one rolling toward you, in the right direction.

And once the debt is gone, there's one question almost nobody plans for. How much is actually enough to keep in the bank? Too little, and one emergency sends you right back here.

That's the next video.
```

## Fact notes (check before recording)
- **Minimum-payment math** (illustrative): $6,000 at 22% APR, a minimum of 1% of the balance + interest (first payment ≈ $170),
  takes ≈ 249 months (20+ years) and ≈ $15,900 total. Keeping the payment fixed at $170: ≈ 58 months, ≈ $9,750 total
  (≈ $6,200 less interest). On the first payment, interest ≈ $110. Card issuers' minimum formulas vary.
- **Minimum payment warning:** required on U.S. statements by the CARD Act of 2009.
- **Anchoring on minimums:** Stewart (2009), "The Cost of Anchoring on Credit-Card Minimum Repayments," *Psychological Science*.
- **Avalanche vs snowball example** (illustrative): $800 at 15%, $2,700 at 27%, $2,500 at 20%; $300 a month, minimums 2%.
  Avalanche ≈ 25 months, ≈ $7,450 total; snowball ≈ 26 months, ≈ $7,780 total; the first card is gone at month 5 (snowball) vs 15 (avalanche).
- **Small wins:** Gal & McShane (2012), "Can Small Victories Help Win the War? Evidence from Consumer Debt Management,"
  *Journal of Marketing Research* (Kellogg, Northwestern).
- **Ostrich effect:** Karlsson, Loewenstein & Seppi (2009); Sicherman et al. (2016) on investors checking accounts less when markets fall.
- **Asking for a lower rate:** LendingTree's cardholder surveys have reported most people who asked got a lower APR. The script says
  "a large share" because the percentage changes each year.
- **Balance transfers:** fees commonly 3–5%; promotional rates end. Not financial advice; keep the disclaimer in the description.

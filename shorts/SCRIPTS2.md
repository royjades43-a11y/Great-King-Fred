## Short 7 — "The Tip Screen Is Designed to Make You Tip More"
**Frame 1:** tag **THE TIP SCREEN IS A TRICK** · a card reader screen with three big buttons: 20% · 25% · 30%. A tiny "custom" button in the corner.

```
That tip screen was designed to make you tip more.

Look at the buttons. Twenty. Twenty-five. Thirty. Your brain treats the middle one as normal... and the custom button is tiny on purpose.

Psychologists call these defaults. Whatever's offered first starts to feel like the right answer.

And here's the crazy part. Researchers studied millions of New York taxi rides. When the screen suggested higher tips, people tipped more on average. Same rides. Different buttons.

So here's the rule. Decide your tip before the screen turns around. Then tap custom.

What's the wildest place a tip screen has asked you to tip? Tell me in the comments.

Because the next time a screen turns around...
```
- **Beats:** the card reader turns toward the viewer → three buttons glow, the middle one pulses → the tiny CUSTOM button with a magnifier → **re-hook:** a yellow taxi, a meter, two screens side by side (15·20·25 vs 20·25·30), a tip jar filling higher on the second → Bean's hand writing "my tip: ___" on a sticky note before the screen turns → sticky-note comment question.
- **Loop:** "...a screen turns around..." → "That tip screen was designed to make you tip more."
- **Pinned comment:** *Self-checkout, a coffee to go, a vending machine... where's the weirdest tip screen you've seen? 👀*
- **Title:** The Tip Screen Is Designed to Make You Tip More 💳 #shorts

## Short 8 — "Your Gym Membership Is Lying to You"
**Frame 1:** tag **$17 PER VISIT?** · a gym card with a dusty cobweb on it, a pair of untouched dumbbells.

```
Your gym membership is costing you more than you think.

When you sign up, you're not paying for the gym you'll use. You're paying for the person you think you'll become.

In a study of almost eight thousand gym members, people on a seventy-dollar monthly plan went about four times a month.

That's more than seventeen dollars a visit... when a ten-visit pass would have cost about ten.

And here's the crazy part. On average, they gave up around six hundred dollars over their membership. Paying for a future self that never showed up.

So here's the rule. Divide what you pay by how often you actually go. If it's more than a single pass, switch.

How many times did you go last month? Be honest in the comments.

Because when you sign up...
```
- **Beats:** a shiny GYM card and a fit, flexing "future Bean" in a thought cloud → real Bean on the sofa → a calendar with only 4 days ticked → a price tag flipping from $70/MONTH to $17/VISIT → a 10-visit pass at $10 → **re-hook:** $600 counting up, money flying into a gym with a ghost of future Bean → a calculator: $70 ÷ 4 → comment sticky note.
- **Loop:** "...when you sign up..." → "Your gym membership is costing you more than you think."
- **Pinned comment:** *Gym, app, magazine, language course... what are you paying for "future you" right now? 😅*
- **Title:** Your Gym Membership Is Lying to You 🏋️ #shorts

## Short 10 — "You Didn't Save $40"
**Frame 1:** tag **YOU DIDN'T SAVE $40** · a receipt with "YOU SAVED $40!" printed in big letters, and a red marker crossing out "SAVED".

```
You didn't save forty dollars. You spent sixty.

When a store says "you saved forty dollars," your brain feels two things. The pain of paying sixty... and the pleasure of a forty-dollar win.

Economists call that second feeling transaction utility. It's the joy of a good deal, separate from whether you needed the thing at all.

And here's the crazy part. That deal feeling is often stronger than the item itself. So we buy things we didn't want... because the price felt like winning.

That's why the original price is always printed right there. It's an anchor.

So here's the rule. Before you buy anything on sale, ask: would I buy this at full price? If not, the deal isn't for you. It's for the store.

What's the best "deal" you regret? Tell me in the comments.

Because when the receipt says you saved forty dollars...
```
- **Beats:** the receipt prints "YOU SAVED $40!" → red marker: SAVED → SPENT $60 → Impulse with heart eyes holding a trophy labelled DEAL → **re-hook:** a seesaw: a small box (THE ITEM) vs a big gold trophy (THE DEAL) → a price tag "WAS $100" with an anchor hanging from it → Bean in front of a closet full of unopened sale bags → the question "FULL PRICE?" with a check and a cross → comment sticky note.
- **Loop:** "...you saved forty dollars..." → "You didn't save forty dollars. You spent sixty."
- **Pinned comment:** *Confess 😂 What's the "deal" you bought that's still sitting in its box?*
- **Title:** You Didn't Save $40 🏷️ #shorts

## Short 11 — "Why Your Old Stuff Feels Worth More"
**Frame 1:** tag **WHY $3 FEELS LIKE $7** · a plain coffee mug with a price tag that reads "$7?!", and Bean hugging it.

```
Why does your old stuff feel worth more than anyone will pay for it?

In a famous experiment, researchers gave coffee mugs to half the people in a room. Then they let them trade.

People who owned a mug wanted about seven dollars to sell it. People without one would only pay about three. Same mug.

It's called the endowment effect. The moment something is yours, your brain counts losing it as a loss... and losses hurt.

And here's the crazy part. It's why your garage is full, your closet is full, and nothing sells at your yard sale.

So here's the rule. Ask: if I didn't own this, would I buy it today, at this price? If the answer's no, let it go.

What's one thing you'll never sell, even though you never use it? Tell me in the comments.

Because the moment something is yours...
```
- **Beats:** Bean hugging a mug labelled MINE → a classroom: mugs dropping on half the desks → two price bubbles: OWNER $7 vs BUYER $3, the mug in the middle → **re-hook:** a garage door rolling up on a mountain of stuff → a yard-sale table with tags nobody touches → the question card "WOULD I BUY IT TODAY?" → Bean handing the mug away, smiling → comment sticky note.
- **Loop:** "...the moment something is yours..." → "Why does your old stuff feel worth more than anyone will pay for it?"
- **Pinned comment:** *Mine's a box of cables I'll "definitely need one day" 😅 What's yours?*
- **Title:** Why Your Old Stuff Feels Worth More 🏠 #shorts

## Short 12 — "Why Surprise Money Disappears So Fast"
**Frame 1:** tag **WHY "FREE" MONEY VANISHES** · a $100 bill with little wings flying out of an open envelope labelled TAX REFUND.

```
Why does surprise money disappear so fast?

A tax refund. A birthday check. Twenty dollars in an old jacket. It's real money... but your brain files it differently.

Psychologists call this mental accounting. Money you worked for goes in the "careful" bucket. Money that just showed up goes in the "fun" bucket.

And here's the crazy part. In experiments, people handed unexpected money tended to spend it more freely than people who had earned the same amount.

But a dollar is a dollar. Your landlord doesn't care where it came from.

So here's the rule. Before you spend a windfall, split it. Half to a goal, half to fun. You still get the joy... and the money actually goes somewhere.

What did you do with your last surprise money? Tell me in the comments.

Because when money just shows up...
```
- **Beats:** an envelope pops open, a winged $100 flies out → three mini windfalls: refund letter, birthday card, a bill in a jacket pocket → Impulse sorting money into two jars: CAREFUL (work) and FUN (surprise) → **re-hook:** two Beans, one handed money and one earning it; the handed one's cash flies away faster → a coin stamped "$1 = $1" and a landlord's mailbox → the money splitting in half into GOAL and FUN jars → comment sticky note.
- **Loop:** "...when money just shows up..." → "Why does surprise money disappear so fast?"
- **Pinned comment:** *Tax refund season is coming 👀 What would you do with an extra $500: save it, spend it, or split it?*
- **Title:** Why Surprise Money Disappears So Fast 💸 #shorts

## Fact notes (for descriptions; check before publishing)
- **Short 7:** Haggag & Paci (2014), "Default Tips," *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*. NYC taxi data from 2009 (13+ million rides): higher suggested defaults raised average tips, though more people also left no card tip. The script says "on average".
- **Short 8:** DellaVigna & Malmendier (2006), "Paying Not to Go to the Gym," *American Economic Review*. 7,752 members; plans over $70/month; about 4.3 visits a month; over $17 per expected visit vs about $10 with a 10-visit pass; about $600 forgone savings.
- **Short 9:** C+R Research subscription survey (2022), 1,000 U.S. adults, reported by CNBC: guessed $86, actual $219, gap $133; 42% had forgotten a subscription they no longer used. It's a 2022 survey; the script says "in a survey" without a year.
- **Short 10:** Richard Thaler (1985), "Mental Accounting and Consumer Choice," *Marketing Science*, where transaction utility is defined. The rest is explanation, not a statistic.
- **Short 11:** Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler (1990), "Experimental Tests of the Endowment Effect and the Coase Theorem," *Journal of Political Economy*. Sellers asked about $7, buyers offered about $3 (rounded). Some later studies found smaller gaps when people were trained in trading.
- **Short 12:** Mental accounting (Thaler); the windfall result is from Arkes et al. (1994), "The Psychology of Windfall Gains," *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*. The script uses "tended to spend more freely". Check the paper before quoting a number.

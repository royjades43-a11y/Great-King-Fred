# PesaBrain viral Shorts 14 & 15 (built with the playbook in `VIRAL.md`)

Both follow the same structure as "The $1.10 Question":
- a question in the first second, with a tempting wrong answer;
- the viewer commits to an answer in the comments before the reveal;
- one "wow" moment, then proof on screen;
- a useful money rule;
- a "send this to..." prompt;
- a last line that loops into the first.

Each is about 110 words, roughly 40–45 s at your read speed after processing.

## Short 14 — "The Penny Question" 🪙
**Why it spreads:** a famous would-you-rather everyone has an instant answer to (the million). Then the penny line on the chart
**explodes** at the end: the wow moment. It's all maths, so every number is checkable, and it turns into a life lesson people
like to share: "don't judge it on day twenty."

**Frame 1:** tag **WHICH ONE DO YOU TAKE?** · on the left, a stack of cash labelled **$1,000,000 TODAY**; on the right, one tiny
shiny penny labelled **1¢ × DOUBLED 30 DAYS**. A big "VS" between them.

**Voiceover** (save as `short14.mp3`):
```
A million dollars today... or one penny that doubles every day for thirty days. Which one do you take?

Pick one. Put it in the comments... before you see the answer.

Most people grab the million. Here's what the penny does.

Day ten? Five dollars. Day twenty? About five thousand. You'd feel like you chose wrong.

Day twenty-eight... it finally passes a million.

Day thirty? Five point three million dollars.

That's compounding. Almost all the growth happens at the very end.

And that's exactly where most people quit. They look at day twenty... and give up.

So whatever you're growing... money, skills, a business... don't judge it on day twenty.

Send this to someone who needs to hear that... and ask them.
```

## Short 15 — "The 50% Trap" 📉
**Why it spreads:** the obvious answer (50%) is wrong, and the real one (100%) surprises people. The escalation ("lose 90%, you
need 900%") is the wow moment. It's useful advice and it makes the sharer look smart ("send this to your friend who's all-in on
one stock").

**Frame 1:** tag **MOST PEOPLE GET THIS WRONG** · a $100 bill split in half with a red **−50%** arrow pointing down, and a big
green **+?%** arrow pointing up.

**Voiceover** (save as `short15.mp3`):
```
Your hundred dollars drops fifty percent. How much does it need to go up to get back to a hundred?

Lock in your answer. Type it in the comments... before I tell you.

Fifty percent? That's what most people say. And it's wrong.

Fifty percent of fifty dollars is only twenty-five. You'd be stuck at seventy-five.

You need a hundred percent. You have to double it... just to get back to where you started.

And it gets worse. Lose ninety percent... and you need nine hundred percent to recover.

Gains and losses aren't equal. One big loss can wipe out years of gains.

That's why the first rule of money isn't "win big." It's "don't lose big." Spread it out. Never bet everything on one thing.

Send this to the friend who's all-in on one stock... and ask them.
```
**Loop:** "...and ask them." → "Your hundred dollars drops fifty percent."

### Short 15 — The 50% Trap
```
If your money drops 50%, how much does it need to gain to get back to even? Not 50%. You need +100%. Lose 90% and you need +900%. This is the math behind "don't lose big", and why diversification matters more than chasing the next big win.

The math:
$100 − 50% = $50
$50 + 50% = $75 (still down $25)
$50 + 100% = $100 (back to even)
$100 − 90% = $10, and $10 needs +900% to get back to $100
Recovery gain needed = loss ÷ (1 − loss)

Spreading money across many investments (for example, a low-cost index fund) is the standard way to avoid one loss wiping you out.
Source: U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, "Beginners' Guide to Asset Allocation, Diversification, and Rebalancing" (investor.gov).

```

The answer is 5 cents (ball 5¢ + bat $1.05 = $1.10). It's the most famous question from the Cognitive Reflection Test, and it shows how your fast, confident brain makes money mistakes like "it's only $5" or "it's on sale".

# PesaBrain Shorts — batch 1: "The Tricks Stores Play on Your Brain"

Six vertical Shorts (1080×1920), each 35–45 seconds, on new topics in the channel's niche: the psychology of spending.
They share the same cast and look as the long videos, but each one is self-contained.

## How every Short is built for engagement
1. **0.0–1.5 s, the hook.** The first frame already shows the headline tag and a surprising visual, with **no intro and no logo**.
   The first spoken line is a claim or a question the viewer instantly wants answered.
2. **Every 1–2 s, a new picture.** Same word-timed beat system as the long videos, but faster.
3. **Mid-video, a "re-hook".** Around 15–20 s, a second surprise ("But here's the crazy part...") stops the swipe-away dip.
4. **The payoff and a one-line rule** the viewer can use today.
5. **A comment question** asked out loud near the end. Comments push Shorts harder than likes.
6. **A loop ending.** The last line runs straight back into the first line, so a rewatch feels seamless and watch-time goes over 100%.
7. **Burned-in captions,** 2–3 words at a time, with the spoken word highlighted. Most people watch Shorts with sound off at first.

Each script below lists: the **on-screen hook** (frame 1), the **voiceover** (formatted for ElevenLabs, like the long videos),
the **beats** (what's on screen), the **loop**, the **pinned comment**, and the **title**.

## Short 1 — "Why $9.99 Tricks Your Brain"
**Frame 1:** tag **$9.99 IS A TRICK** · a gold $9.99 price tag with "= $9?" scribbled next to it · Impulse with heart eyes.

```
Your brain just read nine ninety-nine... as nine dollars.

It's called the left-digit effect. Your brain reads prices left to right... and it stops caring after the first number.

So nine ninety-nine feels like nine. Not ten.

And here's the crazy part. In one famous experiment, a dress sold better at thirty-nine dollars... than at thirty-four. Cheaper didn't win. The nine did.

Stores know this. That's why almost nothing costs a round number.

So here's the rule. When you see a price ending in nine... round it up. Out loud. Ten dollars. Forty dollars. A hundred dollars.

Which store gets you the most with this trick? Tell me in the comments.

Because the next time you see a price ending in ninety-nine...
```
- **Beats:** price tag $9.99 → eye reading left to right, the "9" glowing and the ".99" fading → $9 vs $10 on a balance (feels like 9) → **re-hook:** two dresses, $34 and $39, a sales bar shooting up on $39 → shop shelf of .99 tags → Bean saying "TEN DOLLARS" with a speech bubble → comment question on screen as a sticky note.
- **Loop:** "...ending in ninety-nine..." → "Your brain just read nine ninety-nine as nine dollars."
- **Pinned comment:** *What's the most "$X.99" thing you bought this month? 👇*
- **Title:** Why $9.99 Tricks Your Brain 🧠 #shorts

## Short 2 — "Why Cards Make You Spend More"
**Frame 1:** tag **CARD = SPEND MORE?** · a credit card with a grin vs. a cash wallet that winces.

```
You spend more with a card than with cash. And there's research to prove it.

When you pay with cash, you feel it. The money physically leaves your hand. Psychologists call that the pain of paying.

A card... removes the pain. Tap. Done. Nothing leaves your hand.

But here's the crazy part. In a study at M I T, people bidding on sports tickets offered up to twice as much... when they were told they'd pay by credit card.

Same tickets. Same people. Double the price.

So here's the trick. For your fun spending... use cash, or a debit card with a set weekly amount. Let yourself feel it.

Be honest. Do you spend more with a card? Yes or no in the comments.

Because when paying doesn't hurt...
```
- **Beats:** card vs cash → cash leaving a hand with an "OUCH" → card tap, a sparkle, no pain → **re-hook:** an auction paddle; the "CARD" bid shooting to 2× the "CASH" bid → "SAME TICKETS" stamp → envelope of cash labelled FUN → YES / NO buttons.
- **Loop:** "...when paying doesn't hurt..." → "You spend more with a card than with cash."
- **Pinned comment:** *Yes or no: do you spend more with a card? 💳*
- **Title:** Why Your Card Makes You Spend More 💳 #shorts

## Short 3 — "The Medium Popcorn Trap"
**Frame 1:** tag **THE MEDIUM IS A TRAP** · three popcorn buckets: small $5, medium $8.50, large $9.

```
The medium popcorn isn't for you. It's there to make you buy the large.

Small, five dollars. Large, nine dollars. That's a big jump... so a lot of people pick the small.

Now add a medium for eight fifty. Suddenly the large looks like a steal. Just fifty cents more!

It's called the decoy effect. A bad option, placed there on purpose, so the expensive one looks smart.

And here's the crazy part. When researchers tested this with magazine subscriptions, adding a useless decoy option pushed most people toward the most expensive plan.

So here's the rule. When there are three choices... cover the middle one with your thumb. Then decide.

Where have you seen a decoy like this? Drop it in the comments.

Because the next time you're at the counter...
```
- **Beats:** three buckets with prices → only small and large, Bean picking small → the medium drops in, Impulse whispering "only 50¢ more!" → stamp: DECOY → **re-hook:** subscription menu with a crossed-out decoy row and a big arrow to the top plan → a giant thumb covering the middle bucket → comment sticky note.
- **Loop:** "...at the counter..." → "The medium popcorn isn't for you."
- **Pinned comment:** *Spotted a decoy price lately? Coffee sizes, phone plans, streaming tiers... 👀*
- **Title:** The Medium Popcorn Is a Trap 🍿 #shorts

## Short 4 — "Would You Take This Bet?"
**Frame 1:** tag **WOULD YOU TAKE THIS BET?** · a coin: heads +$150, tails −$100.

```
Flip a coin. Heads, you win a hundred and fifty dollars. Tails, you lose a hundred. Would you take that bet?

Most people say no. Even though, on average, you come out ahead.

Why? Because losing hurts about twice as much as winning feels good. It's called loss aversion, and it was found by Nobel Prize winner Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky.

And here's the crazy part. This is exactly why people panic-sell when the market drops... and why we keep subscriptions we never use. Canceling feels like losing something.

So here's the rule. When a money decision feels scary... ask yourself: is this a real loss, or does it just feel like one?

So... heads or tails? Would you take the bet? Tell me below.

Because if a coin flip says heads...
```
- **Beats:** the coin flipping, +$150 / −$100 → Bean shaking his head NO → a scale where −$100 is twice as heavy as +$150 → "LOSS AVERSION" tag, Prof. Calc → **re-hook:** a red market chart with a SELL button, then a phone of unused subscriptions → a magnifier over "REAL LOSS?" → buttons TAKE IT / NO WAY.
- **Loop:** "...if a coin flip says heads..." → "Flip a coin. Heads, you win a hundred and fifty dollars."
- **Pinned comment:** *Would you take the bet? 🪙 TAKE IT or NO WAY*
- **Title:** Would You Take This Bet? 🪙 #shorts

Free shipping just made you spend twelve dollars to save six.

Your cart is twenty-eight dollars. Shipping is six. But if you hit thirty-five, shipping is free.

So you add a phone case you didn't need... for twelve dollars. Now your total is forty.

You didn't save six dollars. You spent twelve more... to avoid paying six.

Your brain loves the word free. It treats zero as a special number, so a free deal feels much better than the math says it is.

And the store knows it. That's why the threshold is always just a little higher than your cart.

So here's the rule. Before you add something to reach free shipping... ask: would I buy this item on its own today?

Because free shipping just made you...

## Short 6 — "Pay in 4 Is Not Your Friend"
**Frame 1:** tag **PAY IN 4 = A TRAP?** · a $200 jacket shrinking into "4 × $50".

```
Two hundred dollars feels expensive. Four payments of fifty? That feels fine. Same jacket.

That's the trick behind buy now, pay later. Splitting the price makes it feel smaller... so your brain says yes faster.

But here's the crazy part. It's easy to do it again. And again. One pay-in-four for the jacket. One for the shoes. One for the headphones.

Suddenly you're paying fifty here, thirty there, forty over there... every two weeks. And if you miss a payment, there can be late fees.

Each one felt small. Together, they're not.

So here's the rule. Before you split it... ask: would I buy this today, at the full price, with money I already have?

Have you ever stacked pay-in-four plans? Be honest in the comments.

Because two hundred dollars feels expensive...
```
- **Beats:** $200 tag → it splits into four $50 coins, Impulse cheering → **re-hook:** three BNPL plans stacking up like plates (jacket, shoes, headphones) → a calendar with payment dots every two weeks → LATE FEE stamp → one big total receipt → Bean holding cash, "FULL PRICE TODAY?" → comment sticky.
- **Loop:** "...two hundred dollars feels expensive..." → back to the start.
- **Pinned comment:** *How many "pay in 4" plans do you have running right now? 1? 3? More? 👇*
- **Title:** Pay in 4 Is Not Your Friend 🛍️ #shorts

## Fact notes
- **Left-digit / $9 endings:** Anderson & Simester (2003), "Effects of $9 Price Endings on Retail Sales: Evidence from Field Experiments," *Quantitative Marketing and Economics*. Women's clothing sold more at $39 than at $34 or $44.
- **Pain of paying / cards:** Prelec & Simester (2001), "Always Leave Home Without It," *Marketing Letters*. MIT students in a sealed-bid auction for sports tickets bid up to about twice as much when told they'd pay by credit card.
- **Decoy effect:** the Economist subscription example from Dan Ariely, *Predictably Irrational* (2008). With a "print only" decoy at the same price as "print + web", most chose print + web; without it, most chose web only. The popcorn prices in Short 3 are illustrative.
- **Loss aversion:** Kahneman & Tversky (1979), prospect theory. Losses weigh roughly twice as much as equal gains. The coin-flip framing is the classic example Kahneman used.
- **Zero-price effect:** Shampanier, Mazar & Ariely (2007), "Zero as a Special Price," *Marketing Science*. Short 5's prices are illustrative.
- **BNPL:** Short 6 avoids statistics on purpose; it describes the general risk of stacking plans and possible late fees, which vary by provider.

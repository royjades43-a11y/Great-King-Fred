# PesaBrain — the viral Shorts playbook, and one Short built with it

## Part 1 · What the research says (and what's only folklore)

### From YouTube itself
- **Every Short is first shown to a small test audience,** and reach grows or shrinks with how they respond. The main signal
  in a swipe feed is simply: **did they watch, or swipe away?** (YouTube's own analytics report this as "Viewed vs. swiped away",
  [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12220281); YouTube staff interviews summarised by
  [Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/youtube-shorts-algorithm/)).
- **"The algorithm follows the audience, so please the audience."** That's YouTube's Creator Liaison, Rene Ritchie
  ([Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/?p=181751)). There's no secret format; there's what people don't swipe away from.
- **Shorts loop automatically,** so an ending that flows straight back into the start turns into a rewatch.

### From people who study thousands of Shorts
- **Paddy Galloway's study** (about 5,400 Shorts from 33 channels, 3.3 billion views) found the **first second** decides whether
  people stay. The best performers kept roughly **70–90% "viewed"** rather than swiped away
  ([summary](https://www.digitalinformationworld.com/2023/04/paddy-galloway-decoded-youtube-shorts.html)). I reached this through
  summaries, not the original thread.
- **MrBeast's internal production guide** (leaked in 2024, not officially confirmed) says the opening must deliver on the promise
  immediately, and every video needs a **"wow factor"**: one moment people remember and describe to others
  ([Simon Willison's notes](https://simonwillison.net/2024/Sep/15/how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production/)).

### From the science of sharing and curiosity
- **Curiosity is an information gap.** We're most curious when we *think* we know the answer but aren't sure
  (George Loewenstein, "The Psychology of Curiosity", *Psychological Bulletin*, 1994; [PBS summary](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/article/how-to-stimulate-curiosity/)).
  A puzzle the viewer can solve in their head beats a fact they can only receive.
- **High-arousal emotions get shared:** awe, surprise, anger and anxiety. Low-arousal ones like sadness don't. **Useful**
  content also spreads more (Berger & Milkman, "What Makes Online Content Viral?", *Journal of Marketing Research*, 2012;
  [paper](https://cssh.northeastern.edu/pandemic-teaching-initiative/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2020/09/What-Makes-Online-Content-Viral.pdf)).

### What's folklore
Exact thresholds ("lose 40% in 3 s and you're dead"), "post at 6 pm", "faces always win", "the first 200 Shorts don't count".
These come from tool vendors and blogs, not from YouTube or published studies, so I don't build on them.

## Part 2 · The playbook (my upgraded checklist for every PesaBrain Short)

| # | Rule | Why (source above) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Second 0 is the hook, on screen and spoken at the same time.** No intro, no logo, no "hey guys". | Galloway; YouTube's swipe signal |
| 2 | **Open an information gap the viewer thinks they can close.** A question with a tempting answer beats a statement. | Loewenstein |
| 3 | **Make them do something:** answer, guess, commit. A committed answer is personal, so they stay for the reveal. | Curiosity + investment |
| 4 | **Prove it wrong (or right) on screen with one visual "wow" moment.** | MrBeast "wow factor" |
| 5 | **Spike a high-arousal feeling:** surprise ("wait, what?") or mild outrage ("that's rigged"). | Berger & Milkman |
| 6 | **Make it useful:** one money rule they'll use this week. | Berger & Milkman (practical value) |
| 7 | **Give them social currency:** something that makes the *sharer* look smart ("send this to..."). | Berger, *Contagious* |
| 8 | **Ask for the comment before the reveal,** not at the end. People type while they watch. | Engagement while retention is still high |
| 9 | **End mid-sentence into the start,** so the loop is invisible. | YouTube autoplay loop |
| 10 | **30–35 seconds, a new picture every 1.5–2.5 s, captions always on.** | Retention; sound-off viewing |
| 11 | **Every claim checkable.** A viral Short gets fact-checked in the comments. | Channel trust |
| 12 | **Ship an SEO description every time:** the searchable question in the first line, the payoff or the maths spelled out, citations for any study or statistic, a link to the related long video, 3–5 hashtags, and the not-advice line. | Search and suggested traffic; trust |

**Grading a script:** a script must hit 1, 2, 4, 9 and 11, plus at least three of the rest, before it goes to voiceover.

## Part 3 · The viral Short: "The $1.10 Question"

**Why this topic:** It's a famous puzzle (the bat-and-ball problem from Shane Frederick's Cognitive Reflection Test, 2005) that
almost everyone answers wrong *with confidence*. That's the perfect information gap: the viewer is sure, and then they're shown
they're wrong. It's about money, it's quick to do in your head, and it makes the sharer look smart. Comments explode because people
argue about it.

**Checklist score:** hits all 12 rules (description in `VIRAL2.md`).

### Frame 1 (what a scroller sees before any sound)
Tag: **MOST PEOPLE GET THIS WRONG** · a baseball bat and a ball, both with faces, standing on a price tag that says **$1.10 TOTAL**.
The ball holds a little sign: **"How much am I?"**

### Voiceover (paste into ElevenLabs as one file: `short13.mp3`)
```
A bat and a ball cost a dollar ten.

The bat costs one dollar more than the ball.

How much is the ball?

Lock in your answer. Type it in the comments... before I tell you.

Ten cents? That's what almost everyone says. And it's wrong.

If the ball is ten cents, the bat is a dollar ten. Together? A dollar twenty.

The ball is five cents. The bat is a dollar five. Total... a dollar ten.

And don't feel bad. More than half the students at Harvard, MIT and Princeton got this wrong.

Here's why it matters. That fast, confident answer? It's the same voice in your head that says "it's only five bucks"... "it's on sale"... "zero percent interest."

So when a money decision feels obvious... do the math once.

Now send this to the smartest person you know... and ask them.
```
About 125 words, roughly 35 s after the usual processing (pauses trimmed, +5% speed).

### Publishing
- **Title:** The $1.10 Question Most People Get Wrong 🤯 #shorts
- **Pinned comment** (posted *before* people see the answer, so it doesn't spoil it): *What did you say first, be honest 👀 10¢ or 5¢?*
- **Reply to "10 cents" commenters:** *Ball 10¢ + bat $1.10 = $1.20 😅 Try 5¢!* Being right, kindly, keeps the argument going.
- **Description:** *A bat and a ball cost $1.10. The bat costs $1 more than the ball. How much is the ball? (Comment before you watch the answer 👀) Full videos on the psychology of your money → @PesaBrain #shorts #moneytips #psychology*
- **Link:** *The $5 Coffee Myth Is Wrong* ("it's only five bucks").

### Facts
- The bat-and-ball problem: Shane Frederick (2005), "Cognitive Reflection and Decision Making", *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 19(4).
- "More than half of students at Harvard, MIT and Princeton gave the intuitive, incorrect answer" is how Daniel Kahneman reports
  Frederick's result in *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011, chapter 3). The search tools I used couldn't open the original paper's
  table, so please check the paper or the book before quoting the figure anywhere other than the Short itself.

### What to measure after posting
- **Viewed vs. swiped away** in YouTube Studio (Analytics → Content → Shorts). Above about 70% means the hook works.
- **Average percentage viewed above 100%** means the loop works.
- **Comments per 1,000 views** compared with batches 1 and 2. This Short is designed to beat them.
